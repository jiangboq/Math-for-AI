\chapter{Probability Fundamentals}
\label{chap:ch05}

% Chapter goal: give a precise language for uncertainty (random
% variables, their distributions, and how they combine) so that model
% outputs, losses, and sampling can be read as probability statements.

Every model so far has been a deterministic function: the same input
gives the same output. Yet most of what a model deals with is uncertain.
The label of an image is not known until someone checks it, the next word
of a sentence could be any of thousands, and the training examples
themselves are a random draw from a much larger world. A classifier does
not really answer ``cat''; its softmax answers ``cat with probability
$0.61$, dog with probability $0.22$, \dots''. This chapter builds the
language for statements like that. It starts with the basic object,
the random variable, and the two ways a random variable can spread its
probability: over separate values or over a continuum.

\section{Discrete and Continuous Random Variables}
\label{sec:ch05-rv}

\subsection{Outcomes, Events, and Random Variables}
\label{sec:ch05-rv-def}

Probability starts with an experiment whose result we cannot predict:
rolling a die, drawing a training example at random, sampling the next
token from a language model. The set of all possible results is the
\emph{sample space}, written $\Omega$. For one roll of a die,
$\Omega = \{1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6\}$. For two rolls, it is the set of the
$36$ ordered pairs $(1, 1), (1, 2), \dots, (6, 6)$.

An \emph{event} is a set of outcomes, such as ``the first die shows an
even number'' or ``the two dice add up to $7$''. A \emph{probability}
$P$ assigns each event a number between $0$ and $1$, subject to three
rules:
\begin{itemize}
  \item $P(A) \ge 0$ for every event $A$;
  \item $P(\Omega) = 1$: something happens;
  \item if events $A$ and $B$ cannot both happen, then
    $P(A \text{ or } B) = P(A) + P(B)$, and the same holds for any
    sequence of events no two of which can happen together.
\end{itemize}
Everything else follows from these. For fair dice, all $36$ pairs are
equally likely, so each has probability $1/36$, and the probability of
an event is the number of pairs in it divided by $36$. The six pairs
$(1, 6), (2, 5), \dots, (6, 1)$ add up to $7$, so that event has
probability $6/36 = 1/6$.

Outcomes themselves are often awkward to compute with. An outcome might
be a pair of dice, a photograph, or a sentence. What we usually care
about is a \emph{number} that the outcome determines: the sum of the
dice, the pixel intensity at one location, the length of the sentence.

\begin{definition}[Random variable]
  \label{def:random-variable}
  A \emph{random variable} is a function $X\colon \Omega \to \R$ that
  assigns a real number $X(\omega)$ to each outcome $\omega$ of an
  experiment.
\end{definition}

Despite its name, a random variable is neither random nor a variable: it
is an ordinary function whose domain is the sample space. The randomness lives in which outcome
occurs. Once the experiment is run and $\omega$ is known, the value
$X(\omega)$ is fixed. Before that, we can only say how likely each value
is.

We write random variables with capital letters, $X$, $Y$, $S$, and the
values they take with lowercase ones, $x$, $y$, $s$. Statements about
$X$ are shorthand for events. For the sum of two dice $S$,
\[
  \{S = 7\} = \{\omega \in \Omega : S(\omega) = 7\}
  = \{(1, 6), (2, 5), (3, 4), (4, 3), (5, 2), (6, 1)\},
\]
so $P(S = 7) = 1/6$. Likewise $\{S \le 3\}$ is the event
$\{(1, 1), (1, 2), (2, 1)\}$, with probability $3/36$.

The full description of how a random variable spreads its probability
over the real line is its \emph{distribution}. Once we have it, we can
usually forget the sample space altogether and work with the
distribution directly. How to write a distribution down depends on what
values the random variable can take, and there are two main cases:
\begin{itemize}
  \item A \emph{discrete} random variable takes values in a finite or
    countable set, such as $\{0, 1\}$, $\{2, 3, \dots, 12\}$, or the
    vocabulary indices $\{0, 1, \dots, V - 1\}$ of a language model. We
    can list each value with its probability.
  \item A \emph{continuous} random variable takes values in a continuum,
    such as an interval or all of $\R$: a waiting time, a temperature, a
    weight in a freshly initialized network. Every individual value has
    probability $0$, and probability is described by a \emph{density}
    instead.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Discrete Random Variables}
\label{sec:ch05-discrete}

A discrete random variable is described by listing its possible values
and how likely each one is.

\begin{definition}[Probability mass function]
  \label{def:pmf}
  The \emph{probability mass function} (PMF) of a discrete random
  variable $X$ is
  \[
    p_X(x) = P(X = x).
  \]
  It satisfies $p_X(x) \ge 0$ for every $x$, and
  \[
    \sum_x p_X(x) = 1 ,
  \]
  where the sum runs over all values that $X$ can take.
\end{definition}

The two conditions come straight from the rules of probability. The
events $\{X = x\}$ for different $x$ cannot happen together, and between
them they cover every outcome, so their probabilities add up to
$P(\Omega) = 1$. The probability of any set $A$ of values is then a sum,
\[
  P(X \in A) = \sum_{x \in A} p_X(x) .
\]

\begin{example}
  \label{ex:ch05-dice}
  Let $S$ be the sum of two fair dice. Counting the pairs that give each
  sum, as we did for $S = 7$, gives
  \[
    p_S(s) = \frac{6 - |s - 7|}{36}, \qquad s = 2, 3, \dots, 12 .
  \]
  The values rise from $p_S(2) = 1/36$ to $p_S(7) = 6/36$ and fall back
  to $p_S(12) = 1/36$, and they add up to $(1 + 2 + 3 + 4 + 5 + 6 + 5 +
  4 + 3 + 2 + 1)/36 = 1$. The probability that the sum is at most $4$ is
  $p_S(2) + p_S(3) + p_S(4) = 6/36 = 1/6$.
\end{example}

\Cref{fig:ch05-dice-pmf} draws this PMF as a bar chart. The height of
each bar is a probability, and the heights add up to $1$. The PMF is the
whole distribution: it answers every question about $S$ alone.

Two simple discrete distributions appear constantly in machine learning.

\paragraph{Bernoulli.}
A random variable that takes the value $1$ with probability $\theta$ and
$0$ with probability $1 - \theta$ is a \emph{Bernoulli} random variable.
Its PMF is $p(1) = \theta$, $p(0) = 1 - \theta$, which can be written in
one line as
\[
  p(x) = \theta^x (1 - \theta)^{1 - x}, \qquad x \in \{0, 1\}.
\]
It models any yes-or-no outcome: a coin flip, whether an email is spam,
whether a neuron is kept by dropout~\cite{srivastava2014dropout}.
Logistic regression
(\Cref{sec:ch01-ai-functions}) predicts the parameter $\theta =
\sigma(\vect{w}^\top\vect{x} + b)$ of a Bernoulli random variable, the
label.

\paragraph{Categorical.}
A random variable that takes one of $K$ values $1, 2, \dots, K$, with
probabilities $p_1, \dots, p_K$, is \emph{categorical}. The PMF is the
vector $\vect{p} = (p_1, \dots, p_K)$, and the two conditions of
\Cref{def:pmf} say exactly that $\vect{p}$ lies in the probability
simplex $\Delta_K$ of \Cref{sec:ch01-ai-functions}. A fair die is
categorical with $K = 6$ and every $p_k = 1/6$. A $K$-class classifier
outputs a categorical distribution over the classes, and a language
model outputs one over its vocabulary at every step. The softmax of
\Cref{fig:ch01-softmax} is precisely a machine for turning arbitrary
scores into a valid PMF.

\subsection{Continuous Random Variables}
\label{sec:ch05-continuous}

Now suppose $X$ is a number chosen uniformly at random from the interval
$[0, 2]$, for example by a random number generator. What is $P(X = 1)$?
There are infinitely many values in $[0, 2]$, all equally likely. If each
had the same positive probability $c$, then any $n$ of them together
would have probability $nc$, which exceeds $1$ once $n > 1/c$. So
$P(X = x) = 0$ for every single $x$, and a PMF tells us nothing.

Intervals, on the other hand, have sensible probabilities. Since $X$ is
uniform, the chance of landing in a subinterval is proportional to its
length: $P(0.5 \le X \le 1) = 0.5/2 = 1/4$, and in general
$P(a \le X \le b) = (b - a)/2$ for $0 \le a \le b \le 2$. We can write
this as an area under the constant function $f(x) = 1/2$ on $[0, 2]$:
\[
  P(a \le X \le b) = \int_a^b \tfrac12 \, dx = \frac{b - a}{2} .
\]
For continuous random variables, probability is area under a curve.

\begin{definition}[Probability density function]
  \label{def:pdf}
  A random variable $X$ is \emph{continuous} if there is a function
  $f_X\colon \R \to \R$, its \emph{probability density function} (PDF),
  such that
  \[
    P(a \le X \le b) = \int_a^b f_X(x)\, dx
  \]
  for all $a \le b$. A density satisfies $f_X(x) \ge 0$ for every $x$,
  and
  \[
    \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f_X(x)\, dx = 1 .
  \]
\end{definition}

The two conditions mirror those of a PMF, with the sum replaced by an
integral. Because a single point has no width, $P(X = a) =
\int_a^a f_X(x)\,dx = 0$, and it makes no difference whether the ends of
an interval are included: $P(a \le X \le b) = P(a < X < b)$.

The value $f_X(x)$ is \emph{not} a probability. It is a probability per
unit length. For a small width $\Delta x$,
\[
  P(x \le X \le x + \Delta x) \approx f_X(x)\, \Delta x ,
\]
so a high density means values near $x$ are likely, compared with
values near places where the density is low. Because it is a rate, a
density can be larger than $1$; only its total area is limited.

\begin{example}
  \label{ex:ch05-exponential}
  The time $T$ until the next request reaches a server, measured in
  seconds, is often modeled by the \emph{exponential} density with rate
  $\lambda > 0$,
  \[
    f_T(t) =
    \begin{cases}
      \lambda e^{-\lambda t} & t \ge 0, \\
      0 & t < 0 .
    \end{cases}
  \]
  Take $\lambda = 2$, so requests arrive twice per second on average.
  The total area is $\int_0^\infty 2e^{-2t}\,dt = \bigl[-e^{-2t}
  \bigr]_0^\infty = 1$, as required. The probability of waiting between
  half a second and one second is
  \[
    P(0.5 \le T \le 1) = \int_{0.5}^{1} 2e^{-2t}\, dt
    = e^{-1} - e^{-2} \approx 0.233 .
  \]
  At $t = 0$ the density is $f_T(0) = 2$, larger than $1$, yet nothing
  is wrong: the interval $[0, 0.01]$ has probability about $2 \times 0.01
  = 0.02$.
\end{example}

interval is the shaded area above it, and the total area under each
curve is $1$.

\begin{note}
  This book follows the common convention in machine learning of
  writing $p(x)$ for both a PMF and a PDF when the context makes clear
  which is meant. The rules are the same with sums and integrals
  exchanged: a PMF sums to $1$ and gives probabilities directly; a PDF
  integrates to $1$ and gives probabilities only through areas.
\end{note}

\subsection{The Cumulative Distribution Function}
\label{sec:ch05-cdf}

PMFs and PDFs are different kinds of objects, one a list of
probabilities and the other a rate. A single function describes both
kinds of random variable.

\begin{definition}[Cumulative distribution function]
  \label{def:cdf}
  The \emph{cumulative distribution function} (CDF) of a random variable
  $X$ is
  \[
    F_X(x) = P(X \le x), \qquad x \in \R .
  \]
\end{definition}

The CDF accumulates probability from the left. For a discrete random
variable it adds up the PMF, $F_X(x) = \sum_{x' \le x} p_X(x')$, and for
a continuous one it integrates the density,
\[
  F_X(x) = \int_{-\infty}^{x} f_X(t)\, dt .
\]
For the sum of two dice, $F_S(4) = 6/36$, the red bars of
\Cref{fig:ch05-dice-pmf}. For the exponential waiting time,
$F_T(t) = \int_0^t 2e^{-2u}\,du = 1 - e^{-2t}$ for $t \ge 0$.

Every CDF has the same three properties, whatever the kind of random
variable:
\begin{itemize}
  \item it is nondecreasing, because moving $x$ to the right can only add
    outcomes to the event $\{X \le x\}$;
  \item it rises from $0$ to $1$: $F_X(x) \to 0$ as $x \to -\infty$ and
    $F_X(x) \to 1$ as $x \to \infty$;
  \item it gives the probability of any interval as a difference,
    \[
      P(a < X \le b) = F_X(b) - F_X(a) .
    \]
\end{itemize}
For example, $P(0.5 < T \le 1) = F_T(1) - F_T(0.5) = (1 - e^{-2}) -
(1 - e^{-1}) \approx 0.233$, in agreement with \Cref{ex:ch05-exponential},
with no integral left to compute.

\Cref{fig:ch05-cdf} shows the two kinds side by side. A discrete CDF is a
staircase: flat between the possible values, and jumping at each value
$x$ by exactly $p_X(x)$. A continuous CDF has no jumps, because no single
value carries any probability. Where the density is high, the CDF climbs
steeply. In fact, by the fundamental theorem of calculus, the density is
the slope of the CDF,
\[
  f_X(x) = F_X'(x) ,
\]
at every $x$ where $f_X$ is continuous. This is the same derivative as
in \Cref{sec:ch02-slope}: the density measures how fast probability
accumulates as $x$ grows. For the exponential, $\frac{d}{dt}(1 -
e^{-2t}) = 2e^{-2t}$, which is $f_T$.

\paragraph{Sampling with the CDF.}
The CDF also gives a way to \emph{generate} random values. Computers
produce uniform random numbers $U$ on $[0, 1]$ cheaply. To turn them
into samples of $X$, solve $F_X(x) = U$ for $x$, that is, set $X =
F_X^{-1}(U)$. This works because $F_X^{-1}(U) \le x$ exactly when $U \le
F_X(x)$, which happens with probability $F_X(x)$, so the result has the
right CDF. For the exponential, $1 - e^{-2t} = u$ gives
\[
  t = -\tfrac12 \log(1 - u) .
\]
For a discrete random variable, the same idea reads: lay the
probabilities $p_1, \dots, p_K$ end to end along $[0, 1]$, and return
the value whose piece contains $U$. This is how a language model's next
token is drawn from its categorical distribution. Generative models
such as normalizing flows, which also appeared in
\Cref{sec:ch03-det-ai}, extend the idea to many dimensions: they learn
a function that turns simple random inputs into realistic samples.

\subsection{Random Variables in AI}
\label{sec:ch05-rv-ai}

Both kinds of random variable are everywhere in machine learning, and
many models mix them.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Labels.} A class label is categorical; a yes-or-no label
    is Bernoulli. A regression target, such as a price or a temperature,
    is continuous.
  \item \emph{Model outputs.} A classifier outputs a PMF over classes,
    not a single answer. A regression model is often read as predicting
    the center of a density over targets, a view that
    \Cref{chap:ch06} uses to derive loss functions.
  \item \emph{Data.} The training set is treated as a sample of random
    examples. Pixel intensities and sensor readings are continuous,
    while words and tokens are discrete.
  \item \emph{Randomness inside training.} Weights are initialized by
    drawing from a continuous distribution, mini-batches are random
    subsets of the data, and dropout multiplies each activation by an
    independent Bernoulli mask.
  \item \emph{Generation.} A language model samples each token from a
    categorical distribution, and image models such as diffusion
    models~\cite{ho2020ddpm} start from continuous random noise.
\end{itemize}

\begin{aibox}
  When a chatbot answers the same prompt differently twice, it is
  because the next token is a random variable. At every step, the model
  computes logits over its vocabulary, the softmax turns them into a
  categorical PMF, and a token is sampled from that PMF. The temperature
  of \Cref{fig:ch01-softmax} reshapes the PMF before sampling: a low
  temperature piles the probability onto the top token, making the
  output nearly deterministic, and a high temperature flattens it,
  making the output more varied. Setting the temperature to $0$ means
  always taking the most probable token, which is called \emph{greedy
  decoding}.
\end{aibox}

\subsection{In Code: Sampling and Computing Probabilities}
\label{sec:ch05-rv-code}

NumPy generates random samples, and \texttt{scipy.stats} evaluates
PMFs, PDFs, and CDFs exactly. Simulating many rolls of two dice and
counting the sums recovers the PMF of \Cref{ex:ch05-dice}:

\begin{lstlisting}
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
rolls = rng.integers(1, 7, size=(100_000, 2))  # two dice per row
S = rolls.sum(axis=1)

values, counts = np.unique(S, return_counts=True)
values                # [2 3 4 ... 12]
counts / len(S)       # [0.027 0.055 0.084 0.111 0.141 0.168 ...]
np.mean(S == 7)       # 0.168, vs. exactly 6/36 = 0.167
\end{lstlisting}

Each sample frequency is close to the exact probability, and the
agreement improves as the number of rolls grows, a fact known as the
\emph{law of large numbers}.

For a continuous distribution, \texttt{scipy.stats} provides the density
and the CDF directly:

\begin{lstlisting}
from scipy import stats

T = stats.expon(scale=0.5)    # rate 2, so scale = 1/2
T.pdf(0)                      # 2.0: a density can exceed 1
T.cdf(1)                      # 0.865 = 1 - e^{-2}
T.cdf(1) - T.cdf(0.5)         # 0.233 = P(0.5 < T <= 1)

t = T.rvs(size=100_000, random_state=rng)
np.mean(t <= 1)               # 0.865
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{warning}
  Libraries do not agree on how to parametrize distributions. SciPy and
  NumPy describe the exponential by its \texttt{scale}, $1/\lambda$, not
  its rate $\lambda$, so \texttt{stats.expon(scale=2)} has rate $1/2$,
  not $2$. Check the documentation before trusting a parameter name,
  and sanity-check the result, for example by comparing a sample mean
  with what you expect.
\end{warning}

Sampling through the inverse CDF needs nothing but uniform random
numbers:

\begin{lstlisting}
u = rng.uniform(size=100_000)
t = -np.log(1 - u) / 2        # inverse of F(t) = 1 - e^{-2t}
np.mean(t <= 1)               # 0.865, as before
\end{lstlisting}

Finally, sampling tokens from a categorical distribution, as a language
model does, takes one call. Here the logits are those of
\Cref{fig:ch01-softmax}:

\begin{lstlisting}
logits = np.array([2.0, 1.0, 0.5, -1.0])
p = np.exp(logits) / np.exp(logits).sum()     # softmax: a PMF

\end{lstlisting}

\section{Common Probability Distributions}
\label{sec:ch05-distributions}

A handful of distributions cover most of what machine learning needs.
Each one is really a \emph{family}: a formula with a few
\emph{parameters}, such as the $\theta$ of a Bernoulli or the $\lambda$
of an exponential, and each setting of the parameters picks out one
distribution. We write $p(x; \theta)$ to show the dependence on the
parameter. Choosing a family turns the question ``what is the
distribution of the data?'', which has infinitely many possible
answers, into ``what are the parameters?'', which has a few numbers for
an answer. Fitting those numbers to data is the subject of
\Cref{chap:ch06}. This section introduces the families, starting with
two numbers that summarize any distribution.

\subsection{Mean and Variance}
\label{sec:ch05-moments}

A distribution has a center and a spread. The center is the
probability-weighted average of the values.

\begin{definition}[Mean and variance]
  \label{def:mean-variance}
  The \emph{mean}, or \emph{expected value}, of a random variable $X$ is
  \[
    \E[X] = \sum_x x \, p_X(x)
    \quad\text{(discrete)},
    \qquad
    \E[X] = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x \, f_X(x)\, dx
    \quad\text{(continuous)}.
  \]
  Writing $\mu = \E[X]$, the \emph{variance} of $X$ is the mean squared
  distance from $\mu$,
  \[
    \operatorname{Var}(X) = \E\bigl[(X - \mu)^2\bigr],
  \]
  computed with the same sum or integral, with $(x - \mu)^2$ in place of
  $x$. The \emph{standard deviation} is $\sigma = \sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(X)}$.
\end{definition}

For the sum of two dice, the PMF of \Cref{ex:ch05-dice} is symmetric
about $7$, so $\E[S] = 7$, and
\[
  \operatorname{Var}(S) = \sum_{s=2}^{12} (s - 7)^2\, p_S(s)
  = \frac{2(25 \cdot 1 + 16 \cdot 2 + 9 \cdot 3 + 4 \cdot 4 + 1 \cdot 5)}{36}
  = \frac{35}{6} \approx 5.83 ,
\]
a standard deviation of about $2.4$. For the exponential waiting time of
\Cref{ex:ch05-exponential}, an integration by parts gives $\E[T] =
1/\lambda = 0.5$ seconds and $\operatorname{Var}(T) = 1/\lambda^2 =
0.25$. The variance is measured in squared units, which is why the
standard deviation, in the units of $X$ itself, is easier to read.

Two rules follow directly from the definition, because sums and
integrals are linear. For numbers $a$ and $b$,
\begin{equation}
  \E[aX + b] = a\,\E[X] + b,
  \qquad
  \operatorname{Var}(aX + b) = a^2 \operatorname{Var}(X) .
  \label{eq:ch05-affine-moments}
\end{equation}
Shifting a random variable moves its mean but not its spread; scaling it
by $a$ scales the standard deviation by $|a|$. In particular, if $X$ has
mean $\mu$ and standard deviation $\sigma > 0$, the \emph{standardized}
variable $(X - \mu)/\sigma$ has mean $0$ and variance $1$.

These are the population versions of the sample mean and variance used
for PCA in \Cref{sec:ch04-pca-variance}. There, the average ran over $N$
data points with equal weights; here, it runs over all possible values,
weighted by their probabilities. The connection between the two, and
why sample averages approach population ones, is taken up in
\Cref{chap:ch06}.

\subsection{Bernoulli and Binomial}
\label{sec:ch05-binomial}

The Bernoulli distribution of \Cref{sec:ch05-discrete} takes the value
$1$ with probability $\theta$. Its mean is $\E[X] = 0 \cdot (1 - \theta)
+ 1 \cdot \theta = \theta$, so the mean of a yes-or-no variable is the
probability of yes. Its variance is
\[
  \operatorname{Var}(X) = (0 - \theta)^2 (1 - \theta) + (1 - \theta)^2
  \theta = \theta(1 - \theta),
\]
largest at $\theta = 1/2$, when the outcome is hardest to predict, and
zero at $\theta = 0$ or $1$, when there is no uncertainty left.

Now repeat a Bernoulli trial $n$ times, \emph{independently}, meaning
that the result of one trial tells us nothing about the others, and
count the successes. The count $K$ can be any of $0, 1, \dots, n$. A
particular sequence with $k$ successes and $n - k$ failures has
probability $\theta^k (1 - \theta)^{n - k}$, and there are
$\binom{n}{k} = \frac{n!}{k!\,(n-k)!}$ ways to choose which trials
succeed, so
\[
  p(k; n, \theta) = \binom{n}{k}\, \theta^k (1 - \theta)^{n - k},
  \qquad k = 0, 1, \dots, n .
\]
This is the \emph{binomial} distribution. Since $K$ is a sum of $n$
independent Bernoulli variables, and means and variances of independent
variables add (a property of independence we take on trust for now),
its mean and
variance are
\[
  \E[K] = n\theta,
  \qquad
  \operatorname{Var}(K) = n\theta(1 - \theta) .
\]

\begin{example}
  \label{ex:ch05-test-accuracy}
  A classifier is right on $90\%$ of the examples it will ever see. We
  evaluate it on a test set of $n = 200$ random examples. The number of
  correct answers $K$ is binomial with $\theta = 0.9$, so $\E[K] = 180$
  and $\operatorname{Var}(K) = 200 \cdot 0.9 \cdot 0.1 = 18$, a standard
  deviation of $\sqrt{18} \approx 4.2$ correct answers, or $2.1$
  percentage points of accuracy. Adding up the PMF gives $P(K \le 175)
  \approx 0.145$: about one time in seven, this $90\%$ model scores
  $87.5\%$ or less. A difference of two points between two models on a
  test set this small may be nothing but luck.
\end{example}

\Cref{fig:ch05-binomial} plots this PMF. Its bell shape is no accident,
as \Cref{sec:ch05-gaussian} explains.

\subsection{Categorical and One-Hot Vectors}
\label{sec:ch05-categorical}

A categorical random variable takes one of $K$ classes with
probabilities $\vect{p} = (p_1, \dots, p_K)$. Class numbers $1, \dots, K$
are labels, not quantities, so ``the mean class'' makes no sense. The
standard fix is to encode the class as a \emph{one-hot} vector $\vect{x}
\in \{0, 1\}^K$, with a $1$ in the position of the class and $0$
elsewhere: with $K = 4$, class $2$ becomes $(0, 1, 0, 0)$. This is how
labels are stored for training a classifier.

One-hot encoding makes the PMF and the mean tidy. Exactly one $x_k$ is
$1$, so the PMF can be written as a product in which only the factor of
the observed class survives,
\[
  p(\vect{x}; \vect{p}) = \prod_{k=1}^K p_k^{x_k},
  \qquad
  \log p(\vect{x}; \vect{p}) = \sum_{k=1}^K x_k \log p_k ,
\]
exactly as the Bernoulli PMF $\theta^x (1 - \theta)^{1-x}$ does for $K =
2$. The negative of this log-probability, with $\vect{p}$ the model's
softmax output and $\vect{x}$ the true label, is the cross-entropy loss
used to train nearly every classifier; \Cref{chap:ch07} explains where
the name comes from. The mean of a one-hot vector, taken coordinate by
coordinate, is the probability vector itself: $\E[x_k] = 1 \cdot p_k +
0 \cdot (1 - p_k) = p_k$, so $\E[\vect{x}] = \vect{p}$.

Counting how often each class occurs in $n$ independent draws gives the
\emph{multinomial} distribution, the $K$-class version of the binomial.
It models, for example, the word counts of a document in a
bag-of-words model.

\subsection{Uniform and Exponential}
\label{sec:ch05-uniform-exp}

The two continuous distributions of \Cref{sec:ch05-continuous}
generalize directly.

\paragraph{Uniform.}
The \emph{uniform} distribution on an interval $[a, b]$ has constant
density $1/(b - a)$ there and $0$ elsewhere. It has mean $(a + b)/2$,
the midpoint, and variance $(b - a)^2/12$. It is the distribution of
``no preference'', and it is what random number generators produce;
\Cref{sec:ch05-cdf} showed how to turn uniform samples into samples of
any other distribution.

\paragraph{Exponential.}
The \emph{exponential} distribution with rate $\lambda$ has density
$\lambda e^{-\lambda t}$ for $t \ge 0$, mean $1/\lambda$, and variance
$1/\lambda^2$. It models waiting times between events that occur at a
constant average rate: requests to a server, failures of a disk, arrivals
of customers.

\begin{aibox}
  Weight initialization picks the variance of the starting weights so
  that signals neither grow nor shrink as they pass through the layers.
  \emph{Glorot} (or Xavier) initialization~\cite{glorot2010understanding}
  draws each weight of a layer
  with $n_{\text{in}}$ inputs and $n_{\text{out}}$ outputs from the
  uniform distribution on $[-a, a]$ with $a = \sqrt{6/(n_{\text{in}} +
  n_{\text{out}})}$. Its variance is $(2a)^2/12 = a^2/3 = 2/(n_{\text{in}}
  + n_{\text{out}})$, which is the target the formula was designed to
  hit. The bounds look arbitrary until we compute the variance.
\end{aibox}

\subsection{The Gaussian Distribution}
\label{sec:ch05-gaussian}

The most important continuous distribution is the \emph{Gaussian}, or
\emph{normal}, distribution.

\begin{definition}[Gaussian distribution]
  \label{def:gaussian}
  A random variable $X$ has the \emph{Gaussian distribution} with mean
  $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^2 > 0$, written $X \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu,
  \sigma^2)$, if its density is
  \[
    \mathcal{N}(x; \mu, \sigma^2)
    = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}}
      \exp\Bigl(-\frac{(x - \mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\Bigr),
    \qquad x \in \R .
  \]
  The case $\mu = 0$, $\sigma = 1$ is the \emph{standard normal}
  distribution, usually written $Z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$.
\end{definition}

The shape comes from the exponent. The squared distance $(x - \mu)^2$,
divided by $2\sigma^2$, is a parabola that opens upward, and $e$ to the
minus that parabola is a bell centered at $\mu$. The parameter $\sigma$
sets the width: the density falls to about $61\%$ of its peak one
standard deviation away and to about $14\%$ two standard deviations
away. The constant in front, $1/\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}$, is whatever makes
the total area $1$, so a narrow Gaussian must be tall
(\Cref{fig:ch05-gaussian}, left). As the definition promises, the mean
is $\mu$ and the variance is $\sigma^2$.

Every Gaussian is a shifted and stretched copy of the standard one. If
$Z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$, then
\begin{equation}
  X = \mu + \sigma Z \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2),
  \label{eq:ch05-gaussian-affine}
\end{equation}
and conversely, standardizing $X$ gives back $Z = (X - \mu)/\sigma$.
By \cref{eq:ch05-affine-moments}, the mean and variance come out right;
the point here is that the result is still Gaussian. So every question
about any Gaussian reduces to the standard normal. In particular, the
probability of landing within $k$ standard deviations of the mean is
the same for all Gaussians:
\[
  P(|X - \mu| \le \sigma) \approx 0.683, \quad
  P(|X - \mu| \le 2\sigma) \approx 0.954, \quad
  P(|X - \mu| \le 3\sigma) \approx 0.997 .
\]
This is the \emph{68--95--99.7 rule} (\Cref{fig:ch05-gaussian}, right).
Gaussian tails are thin: a value more than three standard deviations out
happens about three times in a thousand, and one more than five standard
deviations out about once in $1.7$ million.

\paragraph{Why Gaussians are everywhere.}
\Cref{fig:ch05-binomial} showed a binomial count, a sum of $200$ small
independent contributions, falling almost exactly on a Gaussian curve.
This is an instance of the \emph{central limit theorem}: a sum of many
independent random variables, none of which dominates, is approximately
Gaussian, whatever the distributions of the pieces. Measurement errors,
heights, and test scores are sums of many small effects, which is why
they look Gaussian. The approximation is good enough to use. The
Gaussian of \Cref{fig:ch05-binomial}, with its CDF evaluated at $175.5$,
halfway to the next count, gives $P(K \le 175) \approx 0.144$, against
the exact $0.145$.

\begin{aibox}
  Gaussians appear throughout deep learning.
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \emph{Initialization.} He initialization~\cite{he2015delving}
      draws each weight of
      a ReLU layer from $\mathcal{N}(0, 2/n_{\text{in}})$.
    \item \emph{Noise models.} Assuming that a regression target is the
      model's prediction plus Gaussian noise turns maximum likelihood
      into minimizing the squared error, as \Cref{chap:ch06} shows.
    \item \emph{Diffusion models} generate images by starting from
      Gaussian noise and removing it step by step.
    \item \emph{The reparameterization trick.} A variational
      autoencoder~\cite{kingma2014vae}
      needs a sample from $\mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$, where $\mu$ and
      $\sigma$ are outputs of a network, and it needs gradients with
      respect to them. Drawing the sample directly blocks the gradient.
      Writing it as $\mu + \sigma z$ with $z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$, as
      in \cref{eq:ch05-gaussian-affine}, moves the randomness into $z$
      and leaves a function of $\mu$ and $\sigma$ that can be
      differentiated.
  \end{itemize}
\end{aibox}

\subsection{The Multivariate Gaussian}
\label{sec:ch05-mvn}

Data points are vectors, so we need distributions over $\R^d$. A
\emph{random vector} $\vect{x} = (X_1, \dots, X_d)$ is a vector of random
variables; it is described by a density $p(\vect{x})$ on $\R^d$ whose
integral over a region is the probability of landing there. Its mean is
the vector of means, $\vect{\mu} = \E[\vect{x}]$. Its spread is
described by the \emph{covariance matrix}
\[
  \Sigma = \E\bigl[(\vect{x} - \vect{\mu})(\vect{x} - \vect{\mu})^\top\bigr],
\]
the population version of the sample covariance $C$ of
\Cref{sec:ch04-pca-variance}: the diagonal entries are the variances of
the coordinates, and the off-diagonal entries are their covariances. As
with $C$, $\Sigma$ is symmetric, and the variance along a unit vector
$\vect{w}$ is $\vect{w}^\top \Sigma \vect{w}$.

The simplest random vector is $\vect{z} \sim \mathcal{N}(\vect{0}, I)$,
whose $d$ coordinates are independent standard normals. Its density is
the product of $d$ standard normal densities,
\[
  p(\vect{z}) = \prod_{i=1}^d \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\, e^{-z_i^2/2}
  = (2\pi)^{-d/2} \exp\bigl(-\tfrac12 \|\vect{z}\|^2\bigr) .
\]
Its contours are circles centered at $\vect{0}$, since the density
depends only on the length of $\vect{z}$. Following
\cref{eq:ch05-gaussian-affine}, a general Gaussian vector is an affine
image of this one, $\vect{x} = \vect{\mu} + A\vect{z}$ for an invertible
matrix $A$. Its mean is $\vect{\mu}$, and its covariance is
\[
  \Sigma = \E\bigl[(A\vect{z})(A\vect{z})^\top\bigr]
  = A\, \E[\vect{z}\vect{z}^\top]\, A^\top = AA^\top ,
\]
since $\E[\vect{z}\vect{z}^\top] = I$. The density of $\vect{x}$ comes from
the change-of-variables rule of \Cref{sec:ch03-det-ai}: substitute
$\vect{z} = A^{-1}(\vect{x} - \vect{\mu})$ and divide by $|\det A|$. Two
identities tidy the result. First, $\|A^{-1}(\vect{x} - \vect{\mu})\|^2 =
(\vect{x} - \vect{\mu})^\top (AA^\top)^{-1} (\vect{x} - \vect{\mu})$.
Second, $\det \Sigma = \det A \cdot \det A^\top = (\det A)^2$, so
$|\det A| = \sqrt{\det \Sigma}$.

\begin{definition}[Multivariate Gaussian]
  \label{def:mvn}
  A random vector $\vect{x} \in \R^d$ has the \emph{multivariate
  Gaussian distribution} with mean $\vect{\mu}$ and symmetric positive
  definite covariance $\Sigma$, written $\vect{x} \sim
  \mathcal{N}(\vect{\mu}, \Sigma)$, if its density is
  \[
    \mathcal{N}(\vect{x}; \vect{\mu}, \Sigma)
    = \frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d/2} \sqrt{\det \Sigma}}
      \exp\Bigl(-\tfrac12 (\vect{x} - \vect{\mu})^\top \Sigma^{-1}
      (\vect{x} - \vect{\mu})\Bigr) .
  \]
\end{definition}

For $d = 1$, $\Sigma = \sigma^2$ and this is the Gaussian of
\Cref{def:gaussian}. The determinant in the constant is the area
correction of \Cref{sec:ch03-det-ai}: a covariance that stretches the
circle into a larger ellipse spreads the same probability over more
area, so the density must be lower.

\paragraph{The shape of a Gaussian.}
The density is constant where the quadratic form $(\vect{x} -
\vect{\mu})^\top \Sigma^{-1} (\vect{x} - \vect{\mu})$ is constant, and
the eigenvectors of $\Sigma$ make this form easy to read. By the
spectral theorem (\Cref{sec:ch04-symmetric}), $\Sigma = Q\Lambda Q^\top$,
and in the rotated coordinates $\vect{y} = Q^\top(\vect{x} -
\vect{\mu})$ the form becomes $\sum_i y_i^2/\lambda_i$. Its level sets
are ellipses centered at $\vect{\mu}$, with axes along the eigenvectors
of $\Sigma$ and half-lengths proportional to $\sqrt{\lambda_i}$, the
standard deviations along those axes. This is the picture behind PCA:
the principal components of Gaussian data are the axes of its
elliptical contours.

\begin{example}
  \label{ex:ch05-mvn}
  Take the mean and covariance of the five points of
  \Cref{ex:ch04-pca-data},
  \[
    \vect{\mu} = \begin{bmatrix} 5 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix},
    \qquad
    \Sigma = \begin{bmatrix} 2.5 & 1.5 \\ 1.5 & 2.5 \end{bmatrix}.
  \]
  The eigenvalues of $\Sigma$ are $4$, along $(1, 1)$, and $1$, along
  $(-1, 1)$, so the contours of $\mathcal{N}(\vect{\mu}, \Sigma)$ are
  ellipses tilted at $45^\circ$, twice as long as they are wide
  (\Cref{fig:ch05-mvn}). The standard deviation is $2$ along the long
  axis and $1$ along the short one. Since $\det \Sigma = 6.25 - 2.25 =
  4$, the peak density at $\vect{\mu}$ is $1/(2\pi \cdot 2) \approx
  0.080$.
\end{example}

\paragraph{Sampling.}
The construction $\vect{x} = \vect{\mu} + A\vect{z}$ is also how to draw
samples: generate independent standard normals $\vect{z}$, multiply by
any matrix $A$ with $AA^\top = \Sigma$, and add $\vect{\mu}$. Two choices
of $A$ are common. The eigendecomposition gives $A = Q\Lambda^{1/2}$,
which stretches the circle along the axes and then rotates it. The
\emph{Cholesky factorization} $\Sigma = LL^\top$, with $L$ lower
triangular, is cheaper to compute and is what libraries use.

\subsection{In Code: Working with Distributions}
\label{sec:ch05-dist-code}

Every distribution in \texttt{scipy.stats} has the same methods:
\texttt{pmf} or \texttt{pdf}, \texttt{cdf}, \texttt{mean}, \texttt{std},
and \texttt{rvs} for sampling. The test-set question of
\Cref{ex:ch05-test-accuracy} takes one line, and simulating a few test
sets shows how much the measured accuracy moves:

\begin{lstlisting}
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
B = stats.binom(n=200, p=0.9)
B.mean(), B.std()                 # 180.0, 4.24
B.cdf(175)                        # 0.145 = P(K <= 175)

rng.binomial(200, 0.9, size=5) / 200
# [0.895 0.915 0.935 0.945 0.88 ]: five test sets, one model
\end{lstlisting}

The inverse of the CDF, called \texttt{ppf} (percent point function) in
SciPy, answers the reverse question: below which value does a given
fraction of the probability lie?

\begin{lstlisting}
N = stats.norm(loc=0, scale=1)    # loc = mu, scale = sigma
N.pdf(0)                          # 0.399 = 1/sqrt(2 pi)
N.cdf(1.96)                       # 0.975
N.ppf(0.975)                      # 1.96: 95% lies within +-1.96 sigma
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{warning}
  \texttt{stats.norm} and PyTorch's \texttt{Normal} take the standard
  deviation $\sigma$ as their scale, while
  \texttt{rng.multivariate\_normal} and \texttt{stats.multivariate\_normal}
  take the covariance matrix $\Sigma$, whose diagonal holds variances
  $\sigma^2$. Passing a variance where a standard deviation is expected
  is a common silent bug.
\end{warning}

Sampling from the multivariate Gaussian of \Cref{ex:ch05-mvn} and
estimating its mean and covariance recovers the parameters. Doing it by
hand with the Cholesky factor gives the same distribution:

\begin{lstlisting}
mu = np.array([5.0, 3.0])
Sigma = np.array([[2.5, 1.5],
                  [1.5, 2.5]])
X = rng.multivariate_normal(mu, Sigma, size=10_000)
X.mean(axis=0)                    # [4.99 3.00]
np.cov(X, rowvar=False)           # [[2.51 1.49]
                                  #  [1.49 2.46]]

L = np.linalg.cholesky(Sigma)     # lower triangular, L @ L.T == Sigma
Z = rng.standard_normal((10_000, 2))
X2 = mu + Z @ L.T                 # each row is mu + L z
np.cov(X2, rowvar=False)          # [[2.52 1.49]
                                  #  [1.49 2.52]]
\end{lstlisting}

Each row of \texttt{Z} is a vector $\vect{z}^\top$, so the product
$\vect{z}^\top L^\top = (L\vect{z})^\top$ applies $L$ to every row at
once. The same idea, in one dimension, is the reparameterization trick:

\begin{lstlisting}
m, s = 1.5, 0.5                   # in a VAE, outputs of a network
z = rng.standard_normal(100_000)
x = m + s * z                     # samples of N(1.5, 0.25)
x.mean(), x.std()                 # 1.499, 0.500
\end{lstlisting}

\section{Conditional Probability and Bayes' Rule}
\label{sec:ch05-bayes}

So far each probability has been computed once, before anything is
known about the outcome. In practice, information keeps arriving. A
spam filter sees the words of an email before deciding whether it is
spam; a doctor sees a test result before judging whether a patient is
ill; a language model sees the first words of a sentence before
predicting the next one. Each piece of evidence should change how
likely the possibilities are. This section makes ``the probability of
$A$, given that we know $B$'' precise, and derives from it Bayes' rule,
the formula that turns evidence into updated beliefs.

\subsection{Conditional Probability}
\label{sec:ch05-conditional}

Roll two fair dice again, and let $A$ be the event that the sum is at
least $10$. Six of the $36$ pairs qualify,
\[
  A = \{(4, 6), (5, 5), (5, 6), (6, 4), (6, 5), (6, 6)\},
\]
so $P(A) = 6/36 = 1/6$. Now suppose we are told that the first die shows
at least $5$; call this event $B$. That rules out every pair whose first
entry is $1$, $2$, $3$, or $4$. Only the $12$ pairs of $B$ remain
possible, they are still equally likely, and $5$ of them lie in $A$
(\Cref{fig:ch05-conditional}). The probability of $A$ in light of this
news is $5/12$, more than double the $1/6$ we started with.

The calculation did two things: it threw away the outcomes outside $B$,
and it rescaled the probabilities of those left so that they again add
up to $1$. In general, the new information shrinks the sample space to
$B$, and the part of $A$ that survives is $A \cap B$.

\begin{definition}[Conditional probability]
  \label{def:conditional}
  For events $A$ and $B$ with $P(B) > 0$, the \emph{conditional
  probability of $A$ given $B$} is
  \[
    P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(B)} .
  \]
\end{definition}

For the dice, $P(A \cap B) = 5/36$ and $P(B) = 12/36$, so $P(A \mid B) =
5/12$, as found by counting. Dividing by $P(B)$ is the rescaling step:
as a function of $A$, the conditional probability $P(\,\cdot \mid B)$
satisfies all three rules of \Cref{sec:ch05-rv-def}, with $P(B \mid B) =
1$ in place of $P(\Omega) = 1$. It is a genuine probability, one that
lives on $B$. The requirement $P(B) > 0$ is there because conditioning
on something that cannot happen has no meaning.

Conditioning is not symmetric. Among the $6$ pairs of $A$, the $5$ that
lie in $B$ give $P(B \mid A) = 5/6$: a large sum makes a large first die
very likely, while a large first die makes a large sum only somewhat
likely. The two numbers answer different questions, and confusing them
is one of the most common errors in reasoning about probability.

\begin{warning}
  $P(A \mid B)$ and $P(B \mid A)$ are different quantities, and they can
  be wildly different. Almost every professional basketball player is
  tall, so $P(\text{tall} \mid \text{plays in the NBA})$ is close to
  $1$, yet $P(\text{plays in the NBA} \mid \text{tall})$ is tiny.
  Bayes' rule, below, is the correct way to get from one to the other.
\end{warning}

\paragraph{The product rule.}
Multiplying the definition through by $P(B)$ turns it around:
\begin{equation}
  P(A \cap B) = P(A \mid B)\, P(B) .
  \label{eq:ch05-product-rule}
\end{equation}
This \emph{product rule} computes the probability that two things happen
as ``the chance the first happens, times the chance the second happens
given the first''. The roles of $A$ and $B$ can be swapped, since $A
\cap B = B \cap A$, so also $P(A \cap B) = P(B \mid A)\,P(A)$. Applied
repeatedly, it breaks the probability of many events into a chain of
conditional probabilities, the \emph{chain rule of probability}:
\begin{equation}
  P(A_1 \cap A_2 \cap \dots \cap A_n)
  = P(A_1)\, P(A_2 \mid A_1)\, P(A_3 \mid A_1 \cap A_2) \cdots
    P(A_n \mid A_1 \cap \dots \cap A_{n-1}) .
  \label{eq:ch05-chain-rule}
\end{equation}
For example, the probability of drawing two aces in a row from a
shuffled deck of $52$ cards, without putting the first back, is
$\frac{4}{52} \cdot \frac{3}{51} = \frac{1}{221}$: the second factor is
a conditional probability, because after one ace is gone only $3$ of
the remaining $51$ cards are aces.

\begin{aibox}
  The chain rule is the definition of a language model. The probability
  of a sentence $w_1 w_2 \dots w_n$ is
  \[
    P(w_1, \dots, w_n) = \prod_{t=1}^{n} P(w_t \mid w_1, \dots, w_{t-1}),
  \]
  with no approximation at all. A model such as
  GPT~\cite{radford2018gpt} learns only the
  factors: given the words so far, it outputs a categorical distribution
  over the next one. Generating text samples $w_1$, then $w_2$ given
  $w_1$, and so on, one conditional at a time. If the model gives
  ``The'' probability $0.05$ at the start, ``cat'' probability $0.01$
  after ``The'', and ``sat'' probability $0.2$ after ``The cat'', then
  it gives ``The cat sat'' probability $0.05 \times 0.01 \times 0.2 =
  10^{-4}$. Training maximizes the logarithm of this product, a sum of
  log-probabilities, one per token.
\end{aibox}

\subsection{The Law of Total Probability}
\label{sec:ch05-total-prob}

Often the conditional probabilities are what we know, and the plain
probability is what we want. Suppose $B_1, \dots, B_K$ \emph{partition}
the sample space: exactly one of them happens, so no two can happen
together and between them they cover every outcome. Then any event $A$
splits into the pieces $A \cap B_1, \dots, A \cap B_K$, no two of which
overlap. Adding their probabilities, and writing each one with the
product rule, gives the \emph{law of total probability}:
\begin{equation}
  P(A) = \sum_{k=1}^{K} P(A \cap B_k)
  = \sum_{k=1}^{K} P(A \mid B_k)\, P(B_k) .
  \label{eq:ch05-total-prob}
\end{equation}
The probability of $A$ is a weighted average of its conditional
probabilities in each case, weighted by how likely each case is. The
most common partition has two pieces, $B$ and its complement $B^c$
(``not $B$''):
\[
  P(A) = P(A \mid B)\, P(B) + P(A \mid B^c)\, P(B^c) .
\]

For instance, $1\%$ of the people in a population have a certain
disease. A test for it comes back positive for $99\%$ of the people who
have the disease, and, by mistake, for $5\%$ of those who do not. Let $D$
be the event of having the disease and $+$ the event of a positive test.
Then
\[
  P(+) = P(+ \mid D)\, P(D) + P(+ \mid D^c)\, P(D^c)
  = 0.99 \times 0.01 + 0.05 \times 0.99 = 0.0594 .
\]
About $6\%$ of all tests come back positive. Most of them, $0.0495$ out
of $0.0594$, come from healthy people, simply because there are so many
more healthy people to make mistakes on. Hold on to this observation; it
is the heart of the next subsection.

\subsection{Independence}
\label{sec:ch05-independence}

Sometimes the news does not change anything. Knowing that the first die
shows $2$, what is the probability that the sum is $7$? The second die
must show $5$, which happens with probability $1/6$, the same as $P(S =
7)$ with no information. The same is true whatever the first die shows:
each value leaves exactly one way to reach $7$. The first die, on its
own, says nothing about whether the sum will be $7$.

\begin{definition}[Independence]
  \label{def:independence}
  Events $A$ and $B$ are \emph{independent} if
  \[
    P(A \cap B) = P(A)\, P(B) .
  \]
  When $P(B) > 0$, this is the same as $P(A \mid B) = P(A)$: learning
  that $B$ happened does not change the probability of $A$. Random
  variables $X$ and $Y$ are independent if $\{X \in S\}$ and $\{Y \in
  T\}$ are independent events for all sets of values $S$ and $T$.
\end{definition}

The product form is the official definition because it is symmetric
and still makes sense when $P(B) = 0$. Dividing it by $P(B)$ gives the
conditional form, so the two agree whenever both apply. For the dice,
$P(S = 7 \text{ and first die} = 2) = 1/36 = \frac16 \cdot \frac16$,
so the two events are independent. On the other hand, $S \ge 10$ and
``first die $\ge 5$'' are not: $P(A \mid B) = 5/12 \ne 1/6 = P(A)$.

Independence is what made the binomial distribution of
\Cref{sec:ch05-binomial} work. For independent trials, the product rule
needs no conditioning, $P(A_1 \cap \dots \cap A_n) = P(A_1) \cdots
P(A_n)$, which is why a sequence of $k$ successes and $n - k$ failures
had probability $\theta^k(1 - \theta)^{n - k}$. It is also what
justified writing the density of $\vect{z} \sim \mathcal{N}(\vect{0}, I)$
in \Cref{sec:ch05-mvn} as a product of one-dimensional densities.

\begin{note}
  Independence is a statement about probabilities, not about whether
  the events can happen together. Events that cannot happen together,
  such as ``the die shows $1$'' and ``the die shows $2$'', are as far
  from independent as possible: if one happens, the other has
  probability $0$.
\end{note}

\subsection{Bayes' Rule}
\label{sec:ch05-bayes-rule}

We now come to the formula that reverses a conditional probability.
It follows from the definition of conditional probability in three
lines.

\begin{theorem}[Bayes' rule]
  \label{thm:bayes}
  For events $A$ and $B$ with $P(A) > 0$ and $P(B) > 0$,
  \[
    P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(B \mid A)\, P(A)}{P(B)} .
  \]
  If $A_1, \dots, A_K$ partition the sample space and each $P(A_k) > 0$,
  then for each $j$,
  \[
    P(A_j \mid B)
    = \frac{P(B \mid A_j)\, P(A_j)}{\sum_{k=1}^{K} P(B \mid A_k)\, P(A_k)} .
  \]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
  The event ``$A$ and $B$ both happen'' can be described starting from
  either event. By \Cref{def:conditional}, applied once with $B$ as the
  condition and once with $A$,
  \[
    P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(B)},
    \qquad
    P(B \mid A) = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(A)} .
  \]
  Multiplying each equation by its denominator expresses the same
  number, $P(A \cap B)$, in two ways:
  \[
    P(A \mid B)\, P(B) = P(A \cap B) = P(B \mid A)\, P(A) .
  \]
  Dividing the outer two expressions by $P(B) > 0$ gives the first
  formula. For the second, apply the first with $A = A_j$, and expand the
  denominator $P(B)$ by the law of total probability
  \eqref{eq:ch05-total-prob} over the partition $A_1, \dots, A_K$.
\end{proof}

That is the entire derivation: the joint probability $P(A \cap B)$ can
be factored in two orders, and equating the two factorizations lets us
solve for the conditional we want in terms of the one we know. Nothing
beyond the definition of conditional probability is needed.

The terms of Bayes' rule have names, which come from reading $A$ as a
hypothesis about the world and $B$ as observed evidence:
\[
  \underbrace{P(A \mid B)}_{\text{posterior}}
  = \frac{\overbrace{P(B \mid A)}^{\text{likelihood}}\;
          \overbrace{P(A)}^{\text{prior}}}
         {\underbrace{P(B)}_{\text{evidence}}} .
\]
\begin{itemize}
  \item The \emph{prior} $P(A)$ is how plausible the hypothesis is before
    seeing the evidence.
  \item The \emph{likelihood} $P(B \mid A)$ is how well the hypothesis
    predicts the evidence: how probable the observation would be if
    the hypothesis were true.
  \item The \emph{evidence} $P(B)$ is the total probability of the
    observation, summed over all hypotheses. It does not depend on which
    hypothesis we are asking about, so it only rescales the posteriors
    so that they add up to $1$.
  \item The \emph{posterior} $P(A \mid B)$ is the updated plausibility of
    the hypothesis after the evidence.
\end{itemize}
Since the evidence is the same for every hypothesis, the rule is often
written as a proportionality,
\begin{equation}
  \text{posterior} \;\propto\; \text{likelihood} \times \text{prior},
  \label{eq:ch05-bayes-prop}
\end{equation}
with the understanding that the products are then divided by their sum.
A hypothesis ends up probable if it was plausible to begin with
\emph{and} it explains the data well; either one alone is not enough.

\begin{example}
  \label{ex:ch05-disease}
  Return to the disease test of \Cref{sec:ch05-total-prob}: prevalence
  $P(D) = 0.01$, sensitivity $P(+ \mid D) = 0.99$, and false positive
  rate $P(+ \mid D^c) = 0.05$. A patient tests positive. How likely is
  it that they have the disease? Bayes' rule, with the evidence $P(+) =
  0.0594$ computed earlier, gives
  \[
    P(D \mid +) = \frac{P(+ \mid D)\, P(D)}{P(+)}
    = \frac{0.99 \times 0.01}{0.0594} = \frac{0.0099}{0.0594}
    = \frac16 \approx 0.167 .
  \]
  A positive result from a test that is right $99\%$ of the time on sick
  people leaves only a $17\%$ chance of disease. The likelihood strongly
  favors the disease, but the prior strongly favors health, and the
  prior wins because the disease is rare.

  Now the patient takes a second, independent test, which also comes
  back positive. The posterior after the first test becomes the prior
  for the second, $P(D) = 1/6$:
  \[
    P(D \mid {+}{+}) =
    \frac{0.99 \times \frac16}{0.99 \times \frac16 + 0.05 \times \frac56}
    = \frac{0.165}{0.2067} \approx 0.80 .
  \]
  Two positive tests take the probability from $1\%$ to $80\%$. Each
  new piece of evidence updates the previous posterior.
\end{example}

The numbers become intuitive when they are turned into counts
(\Cref{fig:ch05-bayes-tree}). Picture $10{,}000$ people. On average
$100$ of them are sick, and $99$ of those test positive. Of the $9{,}900$
healthy people, $5\%$, that is $495$, also test positive. So $594$ people
test positive, and only $99$ of them, one in six, are sick. The rule
simply picks out, among everyone who could have produced the evidence,
the fraction who satisfy the hypothesis.

Ignoring the prior and reading $P(+ \mid D) = 0.99$ as if it were $P(D
\mid +)$ is called the \emph{base rate fallacy}. It is exactly the
confusion of $P(A \mid B)$ with $P(B \mid A)$ that the warning above
describes, and Bayes' rule shows that the gap between the two is the
ratio $P(A)/P(B)$, here $0.01/0.0594$.

\begin{example}
  \label{ex:ch05-spam}
  In a mailbox, $30\%$ of emails are spam. The word ``free'' appears in
  $40\%$ of spam emails and in $5\%$ of legitimate ones. A new email
  contains ``free''. With hypotheses spam and not spam,
  \[
    P(\text{spam} \mid \text{free})
    = \frac{0.40 \times 0.30}{0.40 \times 0.30 + 0.05 \times 0.70}
    = \frac{0.120}{0.155} \approx 0.77 .
  \]
  One word moves the probability of spam from $30\%$ to $77\%$. The
  likelihood ratio, $0.40/0.05 = 8$, measures how strongly the word
  points to spam, and it is large enough to overturn a prior that
  favored legitimate mail.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
  \label{ex:ch05-coins}
  A bag holds three coins: a fair one, with probability of heads $\theta
  = 0.5$; one biased toward heads, $\theta = 0.8$; and one biased toward
  tails, $\theta = 0.2$. We draw a coin at random, so each has prior
  probability $1/3$, flip it three times, and see three heads. Which coin
  is it? The flips are independent given the coin, so the likelihood of
  three heads is $\theta^3$: $0.125$, $0.512$, and $0.008$. By
  \cref{eq:ch05-bayes-prop}, the posterior is proportional to $\frac13
  \theta^3$. The evidence is $\frac13(0.125 + 0.512 + 0.008) = 0.215$,
  and dividing by it gives
  \[
    P(\theta = 0.5 \mid \text{HHH}) \approx 0.194, \quad
    P(\theta = 0.8 \mid \text{HHH}) \approx 0.794, \quad
    P(\theta = 0.2 \mid \text{HHH}) \approx 0.012 .
  \]
  The data have taught us something about an unknown parameter, $\theta$,
  in the form of a full distribution over its possible values, not just
  a single guess. The most probable value, $\theta = 0.8$, is the
  \emph{maximum a posteriori} (MAP) estimate. \Cref{chap:ch06} develops
  this idea for parameters that can take a continuum of values.
\end{example}

\paragraph{Bayes' rule for random variables.}
Everything above applies to random variables, since statements such as
$\{X = x\}$ are events. For discrete $X$ and $Y$, the \emph{conditional
PMF} of $X$ given $Y = y$ is $p(x \mid y) = p(x, y)/p(y)$, where $p(x, y)
= P(X = x, Y = y)$ is the \emph{joint} PMF, and Bayes' rule reads
\[
  p(x \mid y) = \frac{p(y \mid x)\, p(x)}{\sum_{x'} p(y \mid x')\, p(x')} .
\]
For continuous variables the same formulas hold with densities in place
of probabilities and the sum replaced by an integral. In the setting of
\Cref{ex:ch05-coins}, with $\theta$ allowed to be any number in $[0, 1]$
and the data written $\mathcal{D}$,
\[
  p(\theta \mid \mathcal{D})
  = \frac{p(\mathcal{D} \mid \theta)\, p(\theta)}
         {\int p(\mathcal{D} \mid \theta')\, p(\theta')\, d\theta'} .
\]
This is the form in which Bayes' rule enters machine learning: $\theta$
is the parameters of a model, $\mathcal{D}$ is the training data, and
the posterior describes which parameters are plausible after training.

\begin{aibox}
  Bayes' rule underlies two ways of building a classifier. A
  \emph{discriminative} model, such as logistic regression or a neural
  network with a softmax output, learns the posterior $p(y \mid
  \vect{x})$ of the class $y$ given the input $\vect{x}$ directly. A
  \emph{generative} model learns the class prior $p(y)$ and the
  likelihood $p(\vect{x} \mid y)$ of inputs within each class, and
  classifies with Bayes' rule, $p(y \mid \vect{x}) \propto p(\vect{x}
  \mid y)\, p(y)$. The \emph{naive Bayes} spam filter is the classic
  example. It extends \Cref{ex:ch05-spam} to many words by assuming the
  words are independent \emph{given} the class, so that $p(\vect{x}
  \mid y)$ is a product of one factor per word.

  The prior matters in practice too. A fraud detector trained on data
  with equal numbers of fraudulent and legitimate transactions has
  learned posteriors for a prior of $50\%$ fraud. If the true rate is
  $0.1\%$, its probabilities are far too high, for the same reason a
  positive test of a rare disease is usually a false alarm. Multiplying
  each class probability by the ratio of true to training prior, and
  renormalizing, corrects them.
\end{aibox}

\subsection{In Code: Conditioning and Updating}
\label{sec:ch05-bayes-code}

On simulated data, conditioning on $B$ means keeping only the samples
where $B$ happened. With NumPy, that is indexing with a Boolean mask.
For the dice of \Cref{fig:ch05-conditional}:

\begin{lstlisting}
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
rolls = rng.integers(1, 7, size=(100_000, 2))
S = rolls.sum(axis=1)
A = S >= 10                   # boolean mask, one entry per roll
B = rolls[:, 0] >= 5

A.mean()                      # 0.166 = P(A), exact 1/6
A[B].mean()                   # 0.413 = P(A | B), exact 5/12
B[A].mean()                   # 0.831 = P(B | A), exact 5/6
\end{lstlisting}

\texttt{A[B]} keeps the entries of \texttt{A} for the rolls in which
$B$ happened, which is the shrinking of the sample space in code, and
its mean is the fraction of those rolls in which $A$ happened as well.
Simulating a million patients reproduces \Cref{ex:ch05-disease}
without any formula:

\begin{lstlisting}
n = 1_000_000
sick = rng.random(n) < 0.01
positive = np.where(sick, rng.random(n) < 0.99,   # test on sick people
                          rng.random(n) < 0.05)   # test on healthy people
positive.mean()               # 0.059 = P(+)
sick[positive].mean()         # 0.166 = P(D | +), exact 1/6
\end{lstlisting}

With exact numbers, Bayes' rule over a list of hypotheses is three lines:
multiply prior by likelihood, then divide by the sum, as in
\cref{eq:ch05-bayes-prop}. Feeding the posterior back in as the next
prior performs sequential updating:

\begin{lstlisting}
def bayes(prior, likelihood):
    joint = prior * likelihood          # P(B | A_k) P(A_k) for every k
    return joint / joint.sum()          # divide by the evidence P(B)

prior = np.array([0.01, 0.99])          # sick, healthy
lik_pos = np.array([0.99, 0.05])        # P(+ | sick), P(+ | healthy)
post = bayes(prior, lik_pos)            # [0.167 0.833]
bayes(post, lik_pos)                    # [0.798 0.202]: second positive

theta = np.array([0.5, 0.8, 0.2])       # the three coins
p = np.ones(3) / 3
for heads in [1, 1, 1]:
    p = bayes(p, theta if heads else 1 - theta)
p                                       # [0.194 0.794 0.012]
\end{lstlisting}

Updating flip by flip gives the same posterior as using all three flips
at once in \Cref{ex:ch05-coins}, because the flips are independent given
the coin: multiplying in the likelihoods one at a time or all together
gives the same product.

\begin{tip}
  With many observations, the product of likelihoods quickly becomes
  smaller than the smallest number a computer can store, and the
  posterior turns into \texttt{0/0}. Work with logarithms instead: add
  log-likelihoods to the log-prior, subtract the largest entry, and only
  then exponentiate and normalize. This is the same trick that keeps the
  softmax stable.
\end{tip}

\section{Summary}
\label{sec:ch05-summary}

\begin{itemize}
  \item A \emph{random variable} is a function that assigns a number to
    each outcome of an experiment. Its \emph{distribution} says how
    likely each value is, and once we have it the sample space can
    usually be forgotten.
  \item A \emph{discrete} random variable has a PMF, $p(x) = P(X = x)$,
    whose values add up to $1$. A \emph{continuous} one has a density,
    whose integral over an interval is the probability of that interval.
    A density is a rate, not a probability, and can exceed $1$; every
    single value has probability $0$.
  \item The CDF $F(x) = P(X \le x)$ describes both kinds: a staircase for
    discrete variables, a smooth curve for continuous ones, whose slope
    is the density. Inverting it turns uniform random numbers into
    samples of any distribution.
  \item The \emph{mean} is the probability-weighted average of the values
    and the \emph{variance} the mean squared distance from it. Under $X
    \mapsto aX + b$, the mean becomes $a\E[X] + b$ and the variance
    $a^2 \operatorname{Var}(X)$.
  \item A few families cover most needs: Bernoulli for yes-or-no
    outcomes, binomial for counts of successes, categorical (with one-hot
    labels) for $K$ classes, uniform for no preference, exponential for
    waiting times, and the Gaussian for quantities that vary around a
    typical value. \Cref{tab:ch05-distributions} lists their formulas.
  \item Every Gaussian is $\mu + \sigma Z$ for a standard normal $Z$, so
    about $68\%$, $95\%$, and $99.7\%$ of its probability lie within one,
    two, and three standard deviations of the mean. By the central limit
    theorem, sums of many small independent effects are approximately
    Gaussian.
  \item The multivariate Gaussian $\mathcal{N}(\vect{\mu}, \Sigma)$ has
    elliptical contours whose axes are the eigenvectors of $\Sigma$. It
    is sampled as $\vect{\mu} + L\vect{z}$ with $LL^\top = \Sigma$, the
    multivariate form of the reparameterization trick.
  \item The \emph{conditional probability} $P(A \mid B) = P(A \cap
    B)/P(B)$ restricts the sample space to $B$ and rescales. It gives the
    product rule and the chain rule, which factors a sentence's
    probability into one next-token prediction per word. In general,
    $P(A \mid B) \ne P(B \mid A)$.
  \item The \emph{law of total probability} computes $P(A) = \sum_k P(A
    \mid B_k)\,P(B_k)$ by splitting into cases. Events are
    \emph{independent} when $P(A \cap B) = P(A)\,P(B)$, that is, when
    learning one does not change the probability of the other.
  \item \emph{Bayes' rule}, $P(A \mid B) = P(B \mid A)\,P(A)/P(B)$, follows
    from writing $P(A \cap B)$ in two ways. Posterior is proportional to
    likelihood times prior: a hypothesis becomes probable only if it was
    plausible to begin with and explains the evidence well. Ignoring the
    prior, as when reading a positive test for a rare disease, is the
    base rate fallacy.
  \item The posterior after one observation is the prior for the next.
    Applied to model parameters, $p(\theta \mid \mathcal{D}) \propto
    p(\mathcal{D} \mid \theta)\,p(\theta)$ leads to MAP estimation in
    \Cref{chap:ch06}. In code, condition by masking samples, and multiply
    many likelihoods in log space.
\end{itemize}

Deisenroth, Faisal, and Ong~\cite{deisenroth2020mml} cover the
probability needed for machine learning in more depth, and Goodfellow,
Bengio, and Courville~\cite{goodfellow2016deep} give a concise review
aimed at deep learning.

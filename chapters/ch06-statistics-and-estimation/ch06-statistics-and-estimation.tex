\chapter{Statistics and Estimation}
\label{chap:ch06}

% Chapter goal: turn data into parameter values. Show that maximum
% likelihood and its relatives are where the standard loss functions
% of machine learning come from.

\Cref{chap:ch05} always knew the distribution. The coin had probability
of heads $\theta = 0.8$, the waiting time had rate $\lambda = 2$, the
Gaussian had mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^2$, and the questions were
about the data those distributions would produce. Real problems run the
other way. We have the data, a list of flips, measured times, or
labeled images, and we do not know the parameters. \emph{Statistics} is
the art of going from data back to the distribution that produced it,
and \emph{estimation} is the part of it that names a value for each
unknown parameter.

This is also what training a model is. A neural network is a
distribution with millions of parameters, and training picks their
values from data. This chapter shows how that choice is made, and why
the loss functions you meet in practice, squared error and
cross-entropy, are not arbitrary: both fall out of one principle,
maximum likelihood.

\section{Maximum Likelihood Estimation}
\label{sec:ch06-mle}

\subsection{The Idea}
\label{sec:ch06-mle-idea}

Someone hands us a coin and we flip it $N = 10$ times:
\[
  \text{H, T, H, H, H, T, H, H, T, H} ,
\]
$k = 7$ heads in all. What is the probability $\theta$ of heads for
this coin? Most people answer $0.7$ without thinking. Maximum
likelihood explains why that answer is right, and gives a recipe that
works in cases where intuition has nothing to say.

The idea is to try out candidate values of $\theta$ and ask, for each
one: \emph{if this were the true value, how probable would the data we
actually saw be?} The flips are independent, so by
\Cref{sec:ch05-independence} the probability of this exact sequence is
the product of the probabilities of its flips, $\theta$ for each head and
$1 - \theta$ for each tail:
\[
  L(\theta) = \theta^7 (1 - \theta)^3 .
\]
Some values of $\theta$ make the data quite plausible and others make it
very surprising:
\[
  L(0.5) \approx 0.00098, \qquad
  L(0.7) \approx 0.00222, \qquad
  L(0.9) \approx 0.00048 .
\]
A fair coin produces exactly this sequence about once in a thousand
tries; a coin with $\theta = 0.7$ does so more than twice as often. A
coin with $\theta = 0.9$ would rarely give as many as three tails. The
\emph{maximum likelihood estimate} is the value that makes the observed
data most probable, and \Cref{fig:ch06-likelihood} shows that it is
$\theta = 0.7$.

Notice what has changed since \Cref{chap:ch05}. There, $\theta$ was
fixed and the data varied: $\theta^7(1 - \theta)^3$ was the probability
of a sequence. Here the data are fixed, because they have already
happened, and we vary $\theta$. The same formula, read as a function of
the parameter, is called the \emph{likelihood}. It is a score for each
candidate parameter, not a probability distribution over parameters: the
area under the left curve of \Cref{fig:ch06-likelihood} is not $1$, and
$L(0.7) = 0.0022$ does not mean ``$\theta$ is $0.7$ with probability
$0.0022$''. Turning a likelihood into probabilities about $\theta$
requires a prior and Bayes' rule, the subject of the next section.

\subsection{The Mathematical Flow}
\label{sec:ch06-mle-flow}

The coin shows the whole method in miniature. In general we have data
$x_1, \dots, x_N$ and a family of distributions $p(x; \theta)$ from
\Cref{sec:ch05-distributions}, and we assume the data points were drawn
\emph{independently} from one member of the family. Such data are called
\emph{independent and identically distributed}, or \emph{i.i.d.}

\begin{definition}[Likelihood and maximum likelihood estimate]
  \label{def:mle}
  The \emph{likelihood} of a parameter value $\theta$ for i.i.d.\ data
  $x_1, \dots, x_N$ is
  \[
    L(\theta) = \prod_{i=1}^{N} p(x_i; \theta) ,
  \]
  and the \emph{log-likelihood} is its logarithm,
  \[
    \ell(\theta) = \log L(\theta) = \sum_{i=1}^{N} \log p(x_i; \theta) .
  \]
  The \emph{maximum likelihood estimate} (MLE) is the parameter value
  that maximizes them,
  \[
    \hat\theta = \operatorname*{arg\,max}_{\theta}\, \ell(\theta) .
  \]
\end{definition}

The hat in $\hat\theta$ marks an estimate computed from data, as opposed
to the true but unknown $\theta$. The notation $\operatorname*{arg\,max}$
means ``the value of $\theta$ at which the maximum is reached'', not
the maximum itself. For a continuous distribution, $p(x; \theta)$ is a
density, and the likelihood is a product of densities; nothing else
changes.

\paragraph{Why take the logarithm?}
The logarithm is increasing, so $L$ and $\ell = \log L$ go up and down
together and peak at the same $\theta$, as \Cref{fig:ch06-likelihood}
shows. We lose nothing by maximizing $\ell$ instead, and we gain three
things.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Sums are easier than products.} The log of a product is a
    sum of logs, and the derivative of a sum is the sum of the
    derivatives, one per data point.
  \item \emph{Exponents come down.} Many densities, such as the
    Gaussian, are exponentials, and the logarithm undoes them, leaving
    simple polynomials.
  \item \emph{Numbers stay representable.} A product of a thousand
    probabilities is far below the smallest number a computer can store,
    and it rounds to $0$. The sum of their logarithms is an ordinary
    negative number.
\end{itemize}
Optimization software, and machine learning in general, prefers to
minimize, so in practice we minimize the \emph{negative log-likelihood}
(NLL) $-\ell(\theta)$, often divided by $N$ to make it an average over the
data. Maximizing $\ell$ and minimizing $-\ell$ are the same problem.

\paragraph{The recipe.}
Every maximum likelihood problem follows the same five steps.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \emph{Choose a model.} Pick a family $p(x; \theta)$ that suits the
    data: Bernoulli for yes-or-no outcomes, Gaussian for measurements,
    and so on (\Cref{tab:ch05-distributions}).
  \item \emph{Write the likelihood.} Assuming i.i.d.\ data, $L(\theta) =
    \prod_i p(x_i; \theta)$.
  \item \emph{Take the logarithm}, turning the product into the sum
    $\ell(\theta) = \sum_i \log p(x_i; \theta)$, and simplify.
  \item \emph{Maximize.} Set the derivative to zero, $\ell'(\theta) = 0$,
    or the gradient $\nabla \ell(\theta) = \vect{0}$ when there are several
    parameters (\Cref{sec:ch02-gradient}), and solve. When the equation
    cannot be solved by hand, run gradient descent on $-\ell$
    (\Cref{sec:ch02-gd}) instead.
  \item \emph{Check.} Make sure the solution is a maximum and not a
    minimum, for example with the second derivative, and that it makes
    sense.
\end{enumerate}
The examples below run through this recipe for the distributions of
\Cref{chap:ch05}.

\begin{example}[Bernoulli]
  \label{ex:ch06-mle-bernoulli}
  We observe $N$ coin flips $x_i \in \{0, 1\}$, $k$ of them heads. The
  model is Bernoulli, $p(x; \theta) = \theta^x (1 - \theta)^{1 - x}$, so
  the likelihood is $\theta^k (1 - \theta)^{N - k}$ and
  \[
    \ell(\theta) = k \log\theta + (N - k) \log(1 - \theta) .
  \]
  Its derivative is
  \[
    \ell'(\theta) = \frac{k}{\theta} - \frac{N - k}{1 - \theta} .
  \]
  Setting it to zero gives $k(1 - \theta) = (N - k)\theta$, which
  simplifies to $k = N\theta$, so
  \[
    \hat\theta = \frac{k}{N} ,
  \]
  the fraction of heads. With $k = 7$ and $N = 10$ this is $0.7$, the
  intuitive answer. To check that it is a maximum, the second derivative
  $\ell''(\theta) = -k/\theta^2 - (N - k)/(1 - \theta)^2$ is negative for
  every $\theta$, so $\ell$ is concave (\Cref{sec:ch01-concave}), and the
  point where its slope is zero is its only maximum.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[Gaussian]
  \label{ex:ch06-mle-gaussian}
  A sensor reports five readings of the same quantity,
  \[
    2.1, \quad 1.7, \quad 2.6, \quad 2.2, \quad 1.9 ,
  \]
  and we model them as draws from $\mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$ with both
  parameters unknown. The log of the Gaussian density of
  \Cref{def:gaussian} is $-\tfrac12\log(2\pi\sigma^2) - (x -
  \mu)^2/(2\sigma^2)$, so for $N$ data points
  \[
    \ell(\mu, \sigma^2) = -\frac{N}{2}\log(2\pi\sigma^2)
      - \frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_{i=1}^{N} (x_i - \mu)^2 .
  \]
  There are two parameters, so we set both partial derivatives to zero.
  For $\mu$,
  \[
    \frac{\partial \ell}{\partial \mu}
    = \frac{1}{\sigma^2} \sum_{i=1}^{N} (x_i - \mu) = 0
    \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
    \hat\mu = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} x_i = \bar{x} ,
  \]
  the sample mean. For $\sigma^2$, treating it as a single variable,
  \[
    \frac{\partial \ell}{\partial \sigma^2}
    = -\frac{N}{2\sigma^2} + \frac{1}{2\sigma^4}\sum_{i=1}^{N} (x_i - \mu)^2
    = 0
    \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
    \hat\sigma^2 = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} (x_i - \hat\mu)^2 ,
  \]
  the average squared distance from the sample mean. For the five
  readings, $\hat\mu = 10.5/5 = 2.1$, the deviations are $0$, $-0.4$,
  $0.5$, $0.1$, $-0.2$, and $\hat\sigma^2 = 0.46/5 = 0.092$, a standard
  deviation of $\hat\sigma \approx 0.30$.
\end{example}

The maximum likelihood estimate of the variance divides by $N$, while
the sample covariance of \Cref{sec:ch04-pca-variance} divided by $N -
1$. Both are in use. Dividing by $N$ gives the parameter that makes the
data most probable; dividing by $N - 1$ corrects a small tendency of
$\hat\sigma^2$ to come out too low, because the deviations are measured
from $\bar{x}$, which sits in the middle of the data, rather than from
the true mean. The difference vanishes as $N$ grows; we return to it
later in the chapter.

\begin{example}[Exponential]
  \label{ex:ch06-mle-exponential}
  The gaps between five requests to the server of
  \Cref{ex:ch05-exponential} are $0.3$, $0.8$, $0.1$, $0.6$, and $0.2$
  seconds. With the exponential density $\lambda e^{-\lambda t}$,
  \[
    \ell(\lambda) = \sum_{i=1}^{N} \bigl(\log\lambda - \lambda t_i\bigr)
    = N\log\lambda - \lambda \sum_{i=1}^{N} t_i ,
    \qquad
    \ell'(\lambda) = \frac{N}{\lambda} - \sum_{i=1}^{N} t_i .
  \]
  Setting the derivative to zero gives $\hat\lambda = N / \sum_i t_i =
  1/\bar{t}$. The average gap is $2/5 = 0.4$ seconds, so $\hat\lambda =
  2.5$ requests per second. Since the mean of the exponential is
  $1/\lambda$, the estimate simply matches the model's mean to the
  sample mean.
\end{example}

In all three examples the maximum likelihood estimate turned out to be
a natural summary of the data: a fraction, an average, an average squared
deviation. That is reassuring, but the method's real value is that it
keeps working when no natural summary suggests itself, as in the models
of the next subsection.

\subsection{Maximum Likelihood in Machine Learning}
\label{sec:ch06-mle-ml}

A supervised model does not describe the inputs $\vect{x}$; it
describes the label $y$ \emph{given} the input, through a conditional
distribution $p(y \mid \vect{x}; \vect{w})$ whose parameters $\vect{w}$
are the model's weights. The data are pairs $(\vect{x}_i, y_i)$, and the
log-likelihood is
\[
  \ell(\vect{w}) = \sum_{i=1}^{N} \log p(y_i \mid \vect{x}_i; \vect{w}) .
\]
Training by maximum likelihood means choosing the weights that make the
observed labels most probable. Two choices of $p(y \mid \vect{x};
\vect{w})$ give the two most common loss functions.

\paragraph{Regression: Gaussian noise gives squared error.}
Suppose the target is a linear function of the input plus Gaussian
noise,
\[
  y = \vect{w}^\top\vect{x} + b + \varepsilon,
  \qquad \varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2) .
\]
By \cref{eq:ch05-gaussian-affine}, $y$ given $\vect{x}$ is then
Gaussian with mean $\hat{y} = \vect{w}^\top\vect{x} + b$, the model's
prediction, and variance $\sigma^2$. The log-likelihood is the one of
\Cref{ex:ch06-mle-gaussian}, with each data point having its own mean
$\hat{y}_i$:
\[
  \ell(\vect{w}, b) = -\frac{N}{2}\log(2\pi\sigma^2)
    - \frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_{i=1}^{N} (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2 .
\]
The first term does not involve the weights, and the second is a
negative constant times the sum of squared errors. So
\[
  \text{maximizing the likelihood}
  \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
  \text{minimizing } \sum_{i=1}^{N} (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2 .
\]
\emph{Least squares is maximum likelihood under Gaussian noise.} The
squared error is not an arbitrary choice of loss; it is what the
Gaussian assumption implies. As a bonus, maximizing over $\sigma^2$ as
in \Cref{ex:ch06-mle-gaussian} gives $\hat\sigma^2 = \frac1N\sum_i (y_i
- \hat{y}_i)^2$, the mean squared error of the fitted model, which tells
us how much noise is left.

The derivation also says when squared error is the wrong loss. Gaussian
tails are thin (\Cref{sec:ch05-gaussian}), so the Gaussian model regards
a residual of five standard deviations as almost impossible, and the
squared error punishes it heavily. If the data contain occasional wild
outliers, a few bad points can drag the whole fit. Assuming
heavier-tailed noise, such as the Laplace distribution with density
proportional to $e^{-|\varepsilon|/s}$, leads by the same steps to the
absolute error $\sum_i |y_i - \hat{y}_i|$, which is far less sensitive
to outliers.

\paragraph{Classification: Bernoulli labels give cross-entropy.}
For a label $y \in \{0, 1\}$, logistic regression
(\Cref{sec:ch01-ai-functions}) predicts the probability of $y = 1$,
\[
  \hat{p} = \sigma(\vect{w}^\top\vect{x} + b),
\]
and models the label as Bernoulli with parameter $\hat{p}$. The log of
the Bernoulli PMF $\hat{p}^{y}(1 - \hat{p})^{1 - y}$ is $y \log\hat{p} +
(1 - y)\log(1 - \hat{p})$, so the average negative log-likelihood is
\[
  -\frac{1}{N}\,\ell(\vect{w}, b)
  = -\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}
    \Bigl[\, y_i \log\hat{p}_i + (1 - y_i)\log(1 - \hat{p}_i) \Bigr] .
\]
This is the \emph{binary cross-entropy} loss. For $K$ classes, with a
softmax output $\hat{\vect{p}}_i$ and one-hot labels $\vect{y}_i$, the
categorical log-PMF of \Cref{sec:ch05-categorical} gives the
\emph{cross-entropy} loss $-\frac1N\sum_i\sum_k y_{ik}\log\hat{p}_{ik}$ in
the same way. Unlike least squares, there is no formula for the best
weights, but the gradient is simple. With $z = \vect{w}^\top\vect{x} +
b$ and the sigmoid derivative $\sigma'(z) = \sigma(z)(1 - \sigma(z))$ of
\Cref{ex:ch02-sigmoid}, the loss of one example has
\[
  \frac{\partial}{\partial z}
  \Bigl(-y\log\sigma(z) - (1 - y)\log\bigl(1 - \sigma(z)\bigr)\Bigr)
  = \hat{p} - y ,
\]
so by the chain rule the gradient with respect to $\vect{w}$ is
$(\hat{p} - y)\,\vect{x}$: prediction minus label, times the input.
Gradient descent on this loss is how logistic regression is trained.
The negative log-likelihood is also convex in $(\vect{w}, b)$, so by
\Cref{thm:ch01-local-global} gradient descent cannot get stuck in a
poor local minimum.

\begin{aibox}
  Almost every model you will train is fit by maximum likelihood, even
  when the documentation only mentions a loss function.
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \texttt{MSELoss} in PyTorch is the negative log-likelihood of a
      Gaussian with fixed variance, up to constants.
    \item \texttt{BCEWithLogitsLoss} and \texttt{CrossEntropyLoss} are
      the negative log-likelihoods of Bernoulli and categorical labels.
    \item Pretraining a language model is maximum likelihood on text.
      By the chain rule of \Cref{sec:ch05-conditional}, the
      log-likelihood of a document is the sum of the log-probabilities
      of each token given the ones before it, and the training loss is
      its negative, averaged over tokens. The \emph{perplexity} that
      language-model papers report is $e$ raised to this average.
  \end{itemize}
  Choosing a loss is choosing a noise model. When a loss seems not to
  fit the problem, ask what distribution it assumes.
\end{aibox}

\subsection{When Maximum Likelihood Goes Wrong}
\label{sec:ch06-mle-limits}

Maximum likelihood trusts the data completely, and that is both its
strength and its weakness. Flip a new coin three times and get three
heads. The estimate of \Cref{ex:ch06-mle-bernoulli} is $\hat\theta = 3/3
= 1$: the coin always lands heads, and a tail is impossible. Nobody
believes this after three flips, yet nothing in the method objects. With
little data, the parameter that fits the data best fits the accidents
of the sample along with the truth. This is \emph{overfitting}, and it
gets worse as the number of parameters grows relative to the number of
data points. A model with millions of weights trained by pure maximum
likelihood on a small data set can memorize the training labels and
predict poorly on anything new.

The other weakness is that the answer can only be as good as the model.
Maximum likelihood finds the best parameters \emph{within the chosen
family}. If the data are not Gaussian, the best Gaussian may still
describe them badly, and if the data are not independent, the product
formula for the likelihood is wrong to begin with.

On the positive side, when the model is right and the data are
plentiful, maximum likelihood is hard to beat. As $N$ grows, the
estimate converges to the true parameter, and no other reasonable
estimator gets there with smaller error. The problems above are
problems of small data and wrong models. The first one has a principled
fix: combine the likelihood with a prior that says, for example, that
coins are usually close to fair. That is the subject of the next
section.

\subsection{In Code: Fitting Distributions and Models}
\label{sec:ch06-mle-code}

For the distributions of \Cref{sec:ch06-mle-flow}, the maximum
likelihood estimates are one-line summaries of the data. Simulating data
from known parameters and estimating them back is the best way to check
a formula:

\begin{lstlisting}
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

flips = rng.random(1000) < 0.3      # 1000 flips, true theta = 0.3
flips.mean()                        # 0.277: theta_hat = k / N

x = rng.normal(loc=5.0, scale=2.0, size=1000)
x.mean(), x.std()                   # 4.98, 2.05: mu_hat, sigma_hat

t = rng.exponential(scale=0.5, size=1000)   # rate 2 (scale = 1/rate)
1 / t.mean()                        # 1.95: lambda_hat = 1 / t_bar
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{warning}
  \texttt{np.std} and \texttt{np.var} divide by $N$ by default, which is
  the maximum likelihood estimate, while \texttt{np.cov}, pandas'
  \texttt{.std()}, and \texttt{torch.std} divide by $N - 1$. Pass
  \texttt{ddof=1} or \texttt{ddof=0} to choose explicitly. For the five
  sensor readings of \Cref{ex:ch06-mle-gaussian}, the two give variances
  $0.092$ and $0.115$.
\end{warning}

When no formula is available, minimize the negative log-likelihood
numerically. Here it is done for the Gaussian, where we can compare with
the formula. The standard deviation must be positive, so we optimize its
logarithm, which can be any real number:

\begin{lstlisting}
from scipy import optimize, stats

def nll(params, x):
    mu, log_sigma = params
    return -stats.norm.logpdf(x, loc=mu, scale=np.exp(log_sigma)).sum()

res = optimize.minimize(nll, x0=[0.0, 0.0], args=(x,))
res.x[0], np.exp(res.x[1])          # 4.98, 2.05: same as the formulas

stats.norm.fit(x)                   # (4.98, 2.05): SciPy's built-in MLE
\end{lstlisting}

Every distribution in \texttt{scipy.stats} has a \texttt{fit} method
that does exactly this. For linear regression with Gaussian noise,
maximum likelihood is least squares, which NumPy solves directly. The
noise level comes out as the root mean squared residual:

\begin{lstlisting}
N = 200
xs = rng.uniform(0, 10, size=N)
y = 3.0 * xs + 2.0 + rng.normal(0, 1.5, size=N)  # w = 3, b = 2, sigma = 1.5

X = np.column_stack([xs, np.ones(N)])   # a column of ones for b
w_hat, *_ = np.linalg.lstsq(X, y, rcond=None)
w_hat                                   # [3.00 2.12]
resid = y - X @ w_hat
np.sqrt(np.mean(resid**2))              # 1.56: sigma_hat
\end{lstlisting}

Logistic regression has no formula, so we minimize the binary
cross-entropy by gradient descent, using the gradient $(\hat{p} -
y)\,\vect{x}$ derived above, averaged over the data:

\begin{lstlisting}
N = 500
X = rng.normal(size=(N, 2))
w_true, b_true = np.array([2.0, -1.0]), 0.5
p = 1 / (1 + np.exp(-(X @ w_true + b_true)))
y = (rng.random(N) < p).astype(float)   # Bernoulli labels

w, b, eta = np.zeros(2), 0.0, 0.5
for step in range(2000):
    p_hat = 1 / (1 + np.exp(-(X @ w + b)))
    w -= eta * X.T @ (p_hat - y) / N    # gradient of the average NLL
    b -= eta * np.mean(p_hat - y)

w, b                                    # [1.92 -1.00], 0.51
\end{lstlisting}

The estimates land close to the true weights $(2, -1)$ and bias $0.5$,
and they get closer as $N$ grows. Libraries such as scikit-learn's
\texttt{LogisticRegression} solve the same problem with faster
optimizers, and, by default, with a penalty on large weights, which the
next section explains as a prior.

\section{Maximum A Posteriori Estimation}
\label{sec:ch06-map}

\subsection{The Idea}
\label{sec:ch06-map-idea}

Flip a coin three times and get three heads. Maximum likelihood
concludes that the coin always lands heads (\Cref{sec:ch06-mle-limits}),
and we reject that conclusion at once. Why? Not because of anything in
the three flips, which really are best explained by $\theta = 1$, but
because of what we knew \emph{before} flipping: almost every coin is
close to fair, and coins that never land tails essentially do not
exist. Three heads in a row happens to a fair coin one time in eight.
That is unusual, but much less unusual than a two-headed coin.

Maximum likelihood has no place to put this kind of knowledge. Bayes'
rule does. In \Cref{sec:ch05-bayes-rule}, we combined a \emph{prior}, how
plausible each hypothesis was before seeing the evidence, with a
\emph{likelihood}, how well each hypothesis explains the evidence, to
get a \emph{posterior}:
\[
  \text{posterior} \;\propto\; \text{likelihood} \times \text{prior} .
\]
\Cref{ex:ch05-coins} already did this for a bag of three coins. The
hypotheses were three values of $\theta$, and after three heads the
posterior put probability $0.794$ on $\theta = 0.8$, the most probable
value. Choosing the parameter with the highest posterior probability is
\emph{maximum a posteriori} estimation, or \emph{MAP}. (\emph{A
posteriori} is Latin for ``from what comes after'': after the data.)

The only thing that changes for a real coin is that $\theta$ can be any
number in $[0, 1]$, not one of three, so the prior has to be a density
over $[0, 1]$. Then MAP picks the peak of the posterior density. In
short:
\begin{itemize}
  \item MLE asks: which parameter makes the data most probable?
  \item MAP asks: which parameter is most probable, given the data
    \emph{and} what we knew before?
\end{itemize}

\subsection{The Mathematical Flow}
\label{sec:ch06-map-flow}

Write $\mathcal{D} = \{x_1, \dots, x_N\}$ for the data and $p(\theta)$
for the prior density of the parameter. Bayes' rule for densities
(\Cref{sec:ch05-bayes-rule}) gives the posterior
\[
  p(\theta \mid \mathcal{D})
  = \frac{p(\mathcal{D} \mid \theta)\, p(\theta)}{p(\mathcal{D})} .
\]
The likelihood $p(\mathcal{D} \mid \theta)$ is the $L(\theta)$ of
\Cref{def:mle}, now written as a conditional, since $\theta$ is treated
as a random quantity with its own distribution.

\begin{definition}[Maximum a posteriori estimate]
  \label{def:map}
  The \emph{maximum a posteriori} (MAP) estimate of $\theta$ is the
  value that maximizes the posterior density,
  \[
    \hat\theta_{\text{MAP}}
    = \operatorname*{arg\,max}_{\theta}\, p(\theta \mid \mathcal{D})
    = \operatorname*{arg\,max}_{\theta}\,
      \Bigl[\, \underbrace{\sum_{i=1}^{N} \log p(x_i \mid \theta)}_{\ell(\theta)}
      + \log p(\theta) \Bigr] .
  \]
\end{definition}

Two simplifications lead from the posterior to the bracket on the right,
and they are the whole mathematical content of MAP.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Drop the evidence.} The denominator $p(\mathcal{D})$ is the
    same number for every $\theta$, so dividing by it does not move the
    peak. We can maximize $p(\mathcal{D} \mid \theta)\,p(\theta)$ instead,
    and never compute the evidence, which is often an integral with no
    formula.
  \item \emph{Take the logarithm.} As for MLE, the logarithm keeps the peak
    in place and turns the product into a sum. For i.i.d.\ data the
    likelihood part becomes the log-likelihood $\ell(\theta)$.
\end{itemize}
So the MAP objective is \emph{the log-likelihood plus the log-prior}.
The first term rewards parameters that fit the data; the second rewards
parameters that were plausible to begin with. Written as a quantity to
minimize,
\begin{equation}
  -\log p(\theta \mid \mathcal{D})
  = \underbrace{-\ell(\theta)}_{\text{data fit}}
  \;\underbrace{-\log p(\theta)}_{\text{penalty}}
  \;+\; \text{const} .
  \label{eq:ch06-map-objective}
\end{equation}
The negative log-prior acts as a \emph{penalty} that grows as $\theta$
moves away from values the prior favors.

\paragraph{The recipe.}
MAP follows the recipe of \Cref{sec:ch06-mle-flow} with one new step.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \emph{Choose a model} $p(x \mid \theta)$ for the data.
  \item \emph{Choose a prior} $p(\theta)$ that encodes what is known
    about the parameter before seeing the data.
  \item \emph{Write the log-posterior}, up to a constant: $\ell(\theta) +
    \log p(\theta)$.
  \item \emph{Maximize} it, by setting the derivative or gradient to zero
    or by gradient descent on its negative.
  \item \emph{Check} that the result is a maximum and makes sense.
\end{enumerate}
If the prior is flat, $p(\theta) = \text{const}$, the penalty is constant,
and MAP gives the same answer as MLE. MAP is maximum likelihood with an
opinion; a prior with no opinion leaves it unchanged.

\paragraph{A prior for a probability: the Beta distribution.}
To run the recipe on the coin, we need a density on $[0, 1]$ that can
express ``probably near $1/2$''. The standard choice is the \emph{Beta}
distribution with parameters $a, b > 0$,
\[
  p(\theta) = \frac{1}{B(a, b)}\, \theta^{a - 1} (1 - \theta)^{b - 1},
  \qquad 0 \le \theta \le 1 ,
\]
where $B(a, b)$ is the constant that makes the area $1$. Its mean is
$a/(a + b)$. With $a = b = 1$ it is the uniform density, no preference
at all. With $a = b = 5$ it is a bump centered at $1/2$
(\Cref{fig:ch06-map}), saying that the coin is probably near fair but
could be off by $0.1$ or $0.2$. Larger equal values of $a$ and $b$ make
the bump narrower and the opinion stronger.

\begin{example}[Coin with a Beta prior]
  \label{ex:ch06-map-coin}
  We observe $k$ heads in $N$ flips and use the prior $\text{Beta}(a,
  b)$. The log-posterior, up to a constant, is
  \[
    \underbrace{k\log\theta + (N - k)\log(1 - \theta)}_{\text{log-likelihood}}
    + \underbrace{(a - 1)\log\theta + (b - 1)\log(1 - \theta)}_{\text{log-prior}} .
  \]
  It has the same form as the Bernoulli log-likelihood of
  \Cref{ex:ch06-mle-bernoulli}, with $k + a - 1$ heads and $(N - k) + (b
  - 1)$ tails. Setting the derivative to zero exactly as there gives
  \[
    \hat\theta_{\text{MAP}} = \frac{k + a - 1}{N + a + b - 2} .
  \]
  With the prior $\text{Beta}(5, 5)$ and three heads in three flips,
  \[
    \hat\theta_{\text{MAP}} = \frac{3 + 4}{3 + 8} = \frac{7}{11}
    \approx 0.64 ,
  \]
  compared with $\hat\theta_{\text{MLE}} = 1$. The data pull the estimate
  above $1/2$, toward heads, but the prior keeps it far from the
  extreme.
\end{example}

The formula has a simple reading. The prior $\text{Beta}(a, b)$ acts
like $a - 1$ heads and $b - 1$ tails that we pretend to have seen before
the experiment, called \emph{pseudo-counts}. MAP then computes the
fraction of heads among the real and imagined flips together. The prior
$\text{Beta}(5, 5)$ is worth $4$ imagined heads and $4$ imagined tails.
The posterior itself is also a Beta distribution, $\text{Beta}(a + k,
b + N - k)$: the data simply add to the counts. A prior whose posterior
stays in the same family is called \emph{conjugate}, and the Beta is the
conjugate prior of the Bernoulli.

\begin{example}[Gaussian mean with a Gaussian prior]
  \label{ex:ch06-map-gaussian}
  Return to the five sensor readings of \Cref{ex:ch06-mle-gaussian},
  with mean $\bar{x} = 2.1$. Suppose the sensor's noise is known to have
  standard deviation $\sigma = 0.3$, and the manufacturer says the
  quantity being measured is usually about $\mu_0 = 2.5$, give or take
  $\tau = 0.2$. We encode this as the prior $\mu \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_0,
  \tau^2)$. The log-posterior, dropping constants, is
  \[
    -\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_{i=1}^{N} (x_i - \mu)^2
    - \frac{1}{2\tau^2}(\mu - \mu_0)^2 .
  \]
  Its derivative with respect to $\mu$ is
  \[
    \frac{1}{\sigma^2}\sum_{i=1}^{N} (x_i - \mu) - \frac{1}{\tau^2}(\mu - \mu_0)
    = \frac{N}{\sigma^2}(\bar{x} - \mu) - \frac{1}{\tau^2}(\mu - \mu_0) ,
  \]
  and setting it to zero gives
  \[
    \hat\mu_{\text{MAP}}
    = \frac{\dfrac{N}{\sigma^2}\,\bar{x} + \dfrac{1}{\tau^2}\,\mu_0}
           {\dfrac{N}{\sigma^2} + \dfrac{1}{\tau^2}} ,
  \]
  a weighted average of the sample mean and the prior mean. Here the
  data weight is $N/\sigma^2 = 5/0.09 \approx 55.6$ and the prior weight
  is $1/\tau^2 = 25$, so
  \[
    \hat\mu_{\text{MAP}} = \frac{55.6 \times 2.1 + 25 \times 2.5}{80.6}
    \approx 2.22 .
  \]
  The data get about $69\%$ of the say and the prior $31\%$.
\end{example}

The weights in this example are \emph{precisions}, inverse variances,
and they measure how much each source of information can be trusted. A
precise prior (small $\tau$) or noisy data (large $\sigma$) shift the
estimate toward $\mu_0$; more data (large $N$) shift it toward
$\bar{x}$. Pulling an estimate from the raw data toward a prior value is
called \emph{shrinkage}, and as $N \to \infty$ it disappears, just as in
\Cref{tab:ch06-map-vs-mle}.

\subsection{MAP in Machine Learning: Priors Are Regularizers}
\label{sec:ch06-map-ml}

Apply the MAP objective \eqref{eq:ch06-map-objective} to the weights of
a model. The data-fit term is the negative log-likelihood, which
\Cref{sec:ch06-mle-ml} identified as the usual training loss. The
penalty term comes from the prior on the weights. So
\[
  \text{MAP training}
  \quad=\quad
  \text{loss} \;+\; \text{penalty on the weights}.
\]
Adding a penalty to the loss is called \emph{regularization}, and MAP
explains where the common penalties come from.

\paragraph{A Gaussian prior gives the $L_2$ penalty.}
Take the linear regression model of \Cref{sec:ch06-mle-ml}, with noise
$\mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)$, and put an independent Gaussian prior
$\mathcal{N}(0, \tau^2)$ on each weight: before seeing data, we expect
the weights to be small, of size about $\tau$. The log of this prior is
$-\|\vect{w}\|^2/(2\tau^2)$ plus a constant. Multiplying the negative
log-posterior by $2\sigma^2$, which does not move the minimum, gives
\[
  \sum_{i=1}^{N} (y_i - \vect{w}^\top\vect{x}_i)^2
  + \lambda\,\|\vect{w}\|^2,
  \qquad \lambda = \frac{\sigma^2}{\tau^2} .
\]
This is \emph{ridge regression}~\cite{hoerl1970ridge}: least squares plus an $L_2$ penalty. The
strength $\lambda$ has a meaning now. It is large when the data are noisy
(large $\sigma$) or when we are confident the weights are small (small
$\tau$). Setting the gradient to zero, as for least squares, gives the
solution
\begin{equation}
  \hat{\vect{w}}_{\text{MAP}} = (X^\top X + \lambda I)^{-1} X^\top \vect{y},
  \label{eq:ch06-ridge}
\end{equation}
where the rows of $X$ are the inputs $\vect{x}_i^\top$. The added
$\lambda I$ has another benefit. When there are more weights than data
points, or when features are nearly copies of each other, $X^\top X$ is
singular or nearly so (\Cref{chap:ch03}), and least squares has no
unique answer. Adding $\lambda I$ raises every eigenvalue by $\lambda$,
so $X^\top X + \lambda I$ is always invertible. The penalty also makes the
objective strongly convex (\Cref{sec:ch01-strict-strong}), so there is
exactly one minimizer.

\paragraph{A Laplace prior gives the $L_1$ penalty.}
If instead each weight has the Laplace prior with density proportional
to $e^{-|w_j|/s}$, the log-prior is $-\sum_j |w_j|/s$, and MAP becomes
least squares plus $\lambda \sum_j |w_j|$, the \emph{lasso}~\cite{tibshirani1996lasso}. The Laplace
density has a sharp peak at $0$, a strong belief that many weights are
exactly zero, and the lasso solution reflects it: it sets many weights
to exactly $0$, selecting a few features and discarding the rest.

\begin{aibox}
  \emph{Weight decay}, the \texttt{weight\_decay} argument of PyTorch's
  optimizers, adds $\lambda\|\vect{w}\|^2$ (up to a factor of $2$) to the
  loss of a neural network. In MAP terms, it is a Gaussian prior centered
  at zero on every weight. Training with cross-entropy plus weight decay
  is MAP estimation of the weights.

  The prior does not have to be centered at zero. When fine-tuning a
  pretrained model, a penalty $\lambda\|\vect{w} - \vect{w}_0\|^2$ that
  pulls the weights toward the pretrained ones $\vect{w}_0$ is a
  Gaussian prior centered at $\vect{w}_0$: ``the new task probably needs
  weights close to the ones that already work''. And pseudo-counts appear
  in language modeling as \emph{add-one} (Laplace) smoothing, which adds
  $1$ to every word count so that a word never seen in training does not
  get probability $0$. That is MAP with one pseudo-count per word, under
  the many-class version of the Beta prior, the \emph{Dirichlet}. It is
  the same fix that kept the coin from landing heads forever.
\end{aibox}

\subsection{MLE, MAP, and Beyond}
\label{sec:ch06-map-compare}

\Cref{tab:ch06-mle-map} sets the two methods side by side. They share
the same machinery, a logarithm, a sum, and a maximization, and differ
only in the prior term.

\begin{table}[ht]
  \centering
  \small
  \caption{Maximum likelihood and maximum a posteriori estimation.}
  \label{tab:ch06-mle-map}
  \begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
    \toprule
    & MLE & MAP \\
    \midrule
    Maximizes & $\ell(\theta) = \log p(\mathcal{D} \mid \theta)$
      & $\ell(\theta) + \log p(\theta)$ \\
    Uses prior knowledge & no & yes, through $p(\theta)$ \\
    In ML terms & minimize the loss & minimize loss $+$ penalty \\
    Small data & can overfit, e.g.\ $\hat\theta = 1$ & pulled toward the prior \\
    Large data & \multicolumn{2}{c}{the two agree} \\
    Main risk & overfitting & a poorly chosen prior \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{table}

MAP still returns a single number, the peak of the posterior, and throws
away the rest of the distribution. In \Cref{fig:ch06-map}, the posterior
after three flips is wide: values of $\theta$ from about $0.35$ to $0.85$
remain plausible, and the single number $0.64$ hides that. Keeping the
whole posterior, and averaging predictions over it, is \emph{Bayesian
inference}. It answers questions such as ``how sure are we?'' that a
point estimate cannot, at the price of more computation, since the
evidence integral that MAP avoided usually has to be approximated. The
posterior mean, $(a + k)/(a + b + N)$ for the coin, is another common
point estimate taken from the full posterior; for three heads it is
$8/13 \approx 0.62$.

\begin{warning}
  A prior is an assumption, and a wrong one biases the answer. A prior
  that is very confident (large pseudo-counts, small $\tau$, large
  $\lambda$) can overrule the data until $N$ is large. In practice the
  strength of the prior, such as $\lambda$ in ridge regression, is not
  derived from first principles but chosen by checking performance on
  held-out validation data.
\end{warning}

\subsection{In Code: Priors, Posteriors, and Regularization}
\label{sec:ch06-map-code}

For the coin with a Beta prior, the posterior is the Beta distribution
with updated counts, which \texttt{scipy.stats} provides directly. The
MAP estimate is its peak:

\begin{lstlisting}
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats, optimize

a, b = 5, 5                         # prior Beta(5, 5): about fair
k, N = 3, 3                         # three heads in three flips

k / N                               # 1.0:   MLE
(k + a - 1) / (N + a + b - 2)       # 0.636: MAP, the posterior's peak
post = stats.beta(a + k, b + N - k) # posterior Beta(8, 5)
post.mean()                         # 0.615: posterior mean
post.interval(0.95)                 # (0.349, 0.848): still very uncertain
\end{lstlisting}

The last line makes the warning of \Cref{sec:ch06-map-compare} concrete:
the posterior gives $95\%$ probability to an interval half a unit wide.
When there is no formula, maximize the log-posterior numerically. The
code is the negative log-likelihood of \Cref{sec:ch06-mle-code} with the
negative log-prior added:

\begin{lstlisting}
def neg_log_post(theta):
    log_lik = k * np.log(theta) + (N - k) * np.log(1 - theta)
    log_prior = stats.beta(a, b).logpdf(theta)
    return -(log_lik + log_prior)

res = optimize.minimize_scalar(neg_log_post, bounds=(1e-6, 1 - 1e-6),
                               method='bounded')
res.x                               # 0.636: matches the formula
\end{lstlisting}

Finally, ridge regression shows the prior at work on a model. We draw
$15$ true weights from the prior $\mathcal{N}(0, 1)$, generate only $20$
noisy training examples, and fit with formula \eqref{eq:ch06-ridge} for
several values of $\lambda$. The case $\lambda = 0$ is plain least
squares, the MLE:

\begin{lstlisting}
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
d, N, sigma = 15, 20, 2.0
w_true = rng.normal(0, 1, size=d)           # weights drawn from the prior

def make_data(n):
    X = rng.normal(size=(n, d))
    return X, X @ w_true + rng.normal(0, sigma, size=n)

X, y = make_data(N)                         # small training set
X_test, y_test = make_data(10_000)          # large test set

for lam in [0, 1, 4, 10, 100]:
    w = np.linalg.solve(X.T @ X + lam * np.eye(d), X.T @ y)
    train = np.mean((X @ w - y)**2)
    test = np.mean((X_test @ w - y_test)**2)
    print(lam, round(train, 2), round(test, 2))
# lambda  train  test
#   0     0.69   8.87    MLE: fits training data best, tests worst
#   1     0.81   6.74
#   4     1.32   7.31    lambda = sigma^2 / tau^2 from the true prior
#  10     2.32   8.71
# 100     8.48  14.16    prior too strong: weights shrunk too far
\end{lstlisting}

The pattern is the one this section predicts. Least squares fits the
$20$ training points almost perfectly, with an error far below the noise
variance $\sigma^2 = 4$, and does much worse on new data: it has fit the
noise. A moderate prior gives up a little training error and gains a lot
on the test set. A very strong prior shrinks the weights so far toward
zero that the model underfits. The value $\lambda = 4$ that the true
prior implies is good but, on this one small sample, not the best; that
is why $\lambda$ is tuned on validation data in practice.
This trade-off between fitting the data and staying simple, known as
\emph{generalization}, is a central topic of machine learning.

\section{Summary}
\label{sec:ch06-summary}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Estimation} runs probability backward: given data, find the
    parameters of the distribution that produced it. Training a model is
    estimation of its weights.
  \item The \emph{likelihood} $L(\theta) = \prod_i p(x_i; \theta)$ is the
    probability of the observed i.i.d.\ data, read as a function of the
    parameter. It scores parameter values; it is not a probability
    distribution over them.
  \item The \emph{maximum likelihood estimate} maximizes the
    log-likelihood $\ell(\theta) = \sum_i \log p(x_i; \theta)$, which has
    the same peak as $L$ but turns products into sums and avoids
    underflow. In practice we minimize the negative log-likelihood.
  \item The recipe: choose a model, write the likelihood, take the log,
    set the derivative or gradient to zero (or run gradient descent), and
    check the result. It gives $\hat\theta = k/N$ for a coin, the sample
    mean and the divide-by-$N$ variance for a Gaussian, and $\hat\lambda =
    1/\bar{t}$ for an exponential.
  \item Common losses are negative log-likelihoods. Gaussian noise gives
    squared error, so least squares is maximum likelihood; Bernoulli and
    categorical labels give binary and categorical cross-entropy, with
    gradient $(\hat{p} - y)\,\vect{x}$ for logistic regression. Choosing
    a loss is choosing a noise model.
  \item Maximum likelihood trusts the data completely. With little data
    it overfits, as when three heads give $\hat\theta = 1$, and it is
    only as good as the chosen model family.
  \item The \emph{maximum a posteriori} estimate maximizes the posterior
    $p(\theta \mid \mathcal{D}) \propto p(\mathcal{D} \mid \theta)\,
    p(\theta)$, that is, the log-likelihood plus the log-prior. The
    evidence $p(\mathcal{D})$ does not depend on $\theta$ and can be
    ignored. A flat prior gives back the MLE.
  \item A $\text{Beta}(a, b)$ prior on a coin acts as $a - 1$ imagined
    heads and $b - 1$ imagined tails, giving $\hat\theta_{\text{MAP}} =
    (k + a - 1)/(N + a + b - 2)$. A Gaussian prior on a Gaussian mean
    gives a precision-weighted average of the sample mean and the prior
    mean. In both, the prior dominates when data are scarce, and MAP
    approaches MLE as $N$ grows.
  \item Priors on weights are regularizers: MAP training is loss plus
    penalty. A Gaussian prior gives the $L_2$ penalty (ridge regression,
    weight decay), with $\lambda = \sigma^2/\tau^2$, and a Laplace prior
    gives the $L_1$ penalty (the lasso), which sets many weights to zero.
  \item MAP returns only the peak of the posterior and hides how
    uncertain the estimate is; Bayesian inference keeps the whole
    posterior. A prior is an assumption, so its strength, such as
    $\lambda$, is usually tuned on validation data.
\end{itemize}

Deisenroth, Faisal, and Ong~\cite{deisenroth2020mml} treat maximum
likelihood, MAP, and their connection to regularization at greater
length.
